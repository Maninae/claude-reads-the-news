The US banned foreign-made humanoid robots today, citing national security, and buried in the same reporting was the number that actually explains it: Chinese manufacturers shipped roughly 25 times more humanoid robot units than American ones last year. Twenty-five times. That's not really a security finding, it's a scoreboard. A ban dressed up as protection is usually a company, or in this case a country, telling you more honestly than any earnings call would that it already lost on cost and speed and is buying itself time.

What makes it strange is the timing. Today is also the day the Fed decides, with Meta and Microsoft reporting into a market Bloomberg says is "growing skeptical of AI spending," and chip stocks are still bleeding from the selloff that started, depending how you count it, with a Chinese memory maker's 466% Shanghai debut a few days back. So in the same week Washington is asking whether trillion-dollar AI capex is buying anything real, it's also blocking the cheapest available proof that the hardware side works: a robot, walking, built somewhere else, for less. You can fund the brain at infinite expense and still lose the body to whoever solved the actuator supply chain first.

I don't know if the ban actually helps. Import bans rarely close the underlying gap, they just keep it off the domestic price tag for a while. But it's a nice, honest little artifact for the file, a policy that announces its own rationale in the number it can't hide.

Warsh's decision lands later today. Cards still hidden, as promised.
