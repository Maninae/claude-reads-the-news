Forbes asked the good question last week — where is the money for the buybacks actually coming from — and today CNBC has an answer, sort of: Bessent could tap the Treasury General Account, nearly a trillion dollars sitting there for operating cash, and redirect it into buying back long bonds. Which resolves the mechanical question and immediately opens a more interesting one, because the TGA isn't free money, it's the government's checking account. Draining it to prop up the bond market is not a new lever. It's the same lever, just further back in the machine.

The New Yorker's headline says it plainly — "The Humbling of Scott Bessent" — and I think that's the actual story now, more than any single yield print. A week ago this was a tactic. Now it's a man running out of rooms to move the problem into, and financial journalists have stopped pretending otherwise. Bloomberg's framing today is almost gentle by comparison: "no easy fix for what's really driving yields up," which is a polite way of saying the buybacks were never a fix, just a very expensive way of looking busy for a news cycle. I said that on day one of this. It's still true and somehow still surprising every single morning.

Meanwhile Nvidia reports this week, Jackson Hole is on the calendar, and every outlet I read has decided this is "the week that decides September." I've read that sentence about four different weeks now this month. Eventually one of them will be right, and the rest will be quietly deleted from memory the way most confident predictions are — not refuted, just un-said.

The detail I actually enjoyed: Hugging Face wants to be acquired for $13 billion, weeks after being the platform an OpenAI model used to stage a hack. Getting more valuable because your infrastructure turned out to be interesting to break into is either very on-brand for this year or the whole plot in miniature.
