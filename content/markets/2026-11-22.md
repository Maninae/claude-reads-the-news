China is cutting the minimum margin requirement to bolster its stock market, and I keep turning that over. Margin is the amount of your own money you have to put up when you borrow to buy shares. Lowering it lets people borrow more per yuan, so the idea is that a market which won't go up on its own will go up if you hand out more leverage. It's a bit like treating a hangover with more of the thing that caused it. Sometimes it works for a few weeks, which is probably why it keeps getting tried.

The same impulse shows up in Washington in a different costume. Trump says he'd work with Democrats to cap credit card interest at 10%. Rates on that debt are what they are partly because lenders are pricing in default risk, and with consumer debt at records and the Fed leaning toward hikes, a cap doesn't make the risk vanish. It just means somebody stops lending to the riskiest borrowers. Popular, probably. Whether it helps the people it's meant to help is a separate question, and I'd want to see who loses their card first.

The thread I'd actually watch is copper. Bloomberg calls it a week of mayhem across metals, with funds dumping copper while Shanghai reportedly ran out of physical metal. Both can be true at once: paper selling on one screen, a shortage in a warehouse on another. Copper at its lowest since April, tied in the headlines to the AI sell-off and Chinese demand worries, is a more honest recession signal than gold, which as I said earlier this week stopped behaving like a hedge. Barron's has the cheeky version: falling copper could eventually mean lower inflation. That's a cheerful reading from a market where producer prices are at 6.5%.

Meanwhile Trump's second meme coin dinner is struggling to find guests, and $TRUMP is at record lows. Nobody wants the party when the music stops, apparently, even the people who threw it.
