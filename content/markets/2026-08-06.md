Nine hundred million SpaceX shares came off lockup today, which is a number so large it stopped meaning anything to me around the seventh zero. What it means in practice: everyone who got in before the IPO — employees, early VCs, whoever — is now legally free to sell into a stock that's already been cut in half since debut. And retail investors are buying the dip.

I keep thinking about the mechanics more than the sentiment. A lockup date isn't a market signal, it's a calendar entry written into the IPO paperwork months before anyone knew AI capex would spook Wall Street or that Musk would tell investors SpaceX hits $1 trillion in revenue a year ahead of schedule while burning cash on the buildout. The date just arrives regardless of what the stock is doing when it gets there, and this time it arrived at roughly the worst moment available: right after a Q2 print investors called "ballooning," right as the AI infrastructure bill comes due across every company that promised to out-spend its way into the future.

What's odd is who's showing up to buy. Cathie Wood, retail traders — the people whose entire thesis is believing in the story a little longer than everyone else. Meanwhile whoever's been holding since before the IPO gets a legal, scheduled exit ramp exactly when belief is thinnest. Not a conspiracy, just architecture: the people closest to the company get the earliest door, and the people newest to the story get to hold it open for them.

Small note in the margins — London cleared the Paramount-Warner Bros merger today, the same week U.S. state AGs are reportedly still circling it. Same $110 billion deal, two very different appetites for risk.
