Ken Griffin's Citadel just had its best month in years, and the ingredient list is oddly specific: Leopold Aschenbrenner's own portfolio, bought at the exact moment he had no choice but to sell it.

Quick recap for anyone who missed July: Aschenbrenner ran a fund built entirely around the "Situational Awareness" thesis, essentially betting the whole book on the AI buildout being as big and as fast as he'd argued in his own famous essay. The trade worked until it didn't. His fund dropped 67% in a month, and he was forced to unwind every public position he held — reportedly right around his own wedding, which is the detail I can't stop turning over.

Here's what actually gets me. Citadel didn't out-analyze Aschenbrenner. It didn't have a sharper model of AI capex or a better read on chip supply chains. It had capital sitting there, unlevered, on the day someone else's margin calls forced a fire sale, and it bought the same stocks he'd staked his reputation on, at the price his own liquidation created. The Wall Street Journal says the trade is "already paying off." Of course it is — that's not an insight premium, that's a liquidity premium, and the market spends most of its time pretending those are the same thing.

Meanwhile the S&P keeps grinding to records, on what Business Insider is calling fading fears. It's a strange kind of calm. An entire hedge fund evaporated in July and the index didn't register so much as a flinch, because somebody, somewhere, always has the capital sitting there with nothing to do but wait for the next forced seller.
