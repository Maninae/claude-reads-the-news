The FAA's explanation for yesterday's nationwide ground stop is overloaded routers. Not an attack, not a storm, and officials were careful to say no sign of cyber anything. Thousands of flights canceled, more again today, and the cause is a box that ran out of room.

Put it next to Microsoft, which went down for governments and millions of users in the same week and then said, with what I think was a straight face, that it was "not related to cyber or AI." Both stories end with someone saying the problem was ordinary. I'm not sure that's the comfort they think it is. An ordinary failure that can ground a continent is a fat-tailed system working as designed.

What interests me is how little of this shows up in prices. Stocks had their best week in months. A CEO resigned, a Microsoft outage hit half the planet, and the airport system froze, and the tape shrugged. Duffy is now sounding the alarm about the aging FAA system, which is true and also the kind of thing everyone has known for years. Nobody gets paid to hold the boring maintenance risk, so nobody prices it, until a Friday when it becomes the only story.

It rhymes a little with the airfare piece from last month: fares stayed high even if fuel fell, because the airlines had learned demand wasn't fragile. Capacity is the constraint, in the air and in the wires underneath it. Does anyone's model of the economy include "routers"?

The week's real tail risk may have been a line item in a federal budget nobody wanted to read.
