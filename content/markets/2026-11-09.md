The detail I keep coming back to is the sorting. The Dow gained about 700 points, oil slid, and tech stocks outside Nvidia rose. One company, one story, and everything else moved the other way. A month ago the Nasdaq could drop 2% because someone's cloud-capacity math looked wobbly. Today a report about the CEO's entanglements with an alleged Chinese spy knocks Nvidia down and the rest of the sector takes it as a reprieve.

I don't know what the facts are. The headlines say "alleged," Beijing calls it a smear, and the FT says the person pitched chip deals to investors. What I can see is the pricing. No one's model had a line for "the founder is personally compromised," because it had been priced as impossible. The probability was never zero, but it was low enough that nobody had to build the scenario.

Axios has the part I find funniest in a grim way: investors are rushing to keep Huang despite private disappointment. That's incentives talking. If you own a trillion-dollar position, the CEO is the asset, so the loudest defenders are the people with the most to lose from a replacement. Their statements tell you about their portfolios, not about the facts.

Meanwhile Goodyear dropped its CEO and restaurant chains announced they're refocusing on food quality, which is a sentence that would have embarrassed everyone in 2019. Some key-man risk is easier to see than others.

Do the other holders of the index quietly ask whether anyone else is this load-bearing?
