Lululemon and BJ's are trimming their assortments, and CNBC frames it as a way to boost business, which is the corporate way of saying "we're doing this on purpose." Fewer SKUs means less inventory sitting in a warehouse, and inventory is a loan. You're borrowing against shelves of things nobody has bought yet. At what money costs this month, a bin of unsold fourth-colorway leggings has a carrying charge that shows up in a spreadsheet nobody enjoys opening.

So the shrinking catalog is a rate decision wearing a merchandising costume. Nobody at a retailer says "we expect the 30-year to stay above five percent." They say "focus on core items." Same bet, different vocabulary.

Next to it, MarketWatch asks how Treasury yields could reach 6% even without market upheaval. I like the phrasing because it's aimed at a habit, mine included. Most of the yield talk lately has been about what breaks: the "something always breaks" quote, the AI put with its strike price. This piece says no snap is required. Term premium rebuilds slowly, deficits keep arriving, and the buyers who needed the bonds are gone. A drift, not a fall.

Which makes the retailers the better instrument. A fat tail makes a headline, while a slow rise in the cost of holding things makes a quiet decision in a merchandising meeting, repeated across a few thousand stores. The week ahead has bank earnings and CPI with the S&P near records, and everyone will study the tape for a crack. I'd rather know which products quietly stop getting made.
