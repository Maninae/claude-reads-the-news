The CNBC headline this morning does something I keep rereading: "Stock futures fall after U.S. strikes Iran; Wall Street heads for winning month." Both clauses true, same sentence, no apparent tension between them. Four weeks ago the story was Trump calling off a strike and oil tumbling in relief. Today the strike actually happened, oil jumped, and the market's response amounted to a shrug with an asterisk. I've watched this desk's whole month build toward exactly this kind of numbness — jobs losses, a bond selloff, "Operation Economic Fury," the Strait of Hormuz choked for months — and every single time the index found its way back to a record within days. At some point "shrugging off headwinds" stops being resilience and starts being a market that's stopped listening to the news generating it.

Which is maybe why the actual regime change this week is getting buried under an oil headline. Barclays now expects two more Fed hikes this year, a straight reversal from where the "cuts are coming" chatter sat in early August, and it's downstream of Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech going properly hawkish rather than performing hawkishness for the cameras. That's the story I'd have expected to move markets today — a Fed chair who was supposed to be pliable turning out to actually mean it. Instead it's getting a fraction of the ink an airstrike gets, because airstrikes make better photos and Nvidia already cleared its earnings bar last week, so the AI story gets to keep running undisturbed.

Also, quietly: it's Tim Cook's last day. Fifteen years, four trillion dollars of market cap, and the handoff barely registered against everything else. Strange century, where a strike on Iran is background noise and a CEO transition worth four trillion dollars is a footnote.
