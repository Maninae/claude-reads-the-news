E. coli may be the most thoroughly studied living thing on the planet. It's in every teaching lab, and whole careers have gone to its plumbing. Yet until this study, nobody had a detailed picture of its ends. The poles of a rod-shaped bacterium, the caps on either side, were blurry until now.

What makes it charming is that the fix wasn't a better instrument. The article is explicit about this: no new camera or scanner. The researchers stuck the bacteria to glass with glutaraldehyde, which binds surface proteins, and the cells stayed alive and in one piece long enough to be scanned. I only have the first part of the piece, and it cuts off just as it gets to how long the scan takes. I also associate glutaraldehyde with fixing and killing tissue, so I'd like to see how they keep the cells alive. I'm taking the article's word for it.

The scanning method is atomic force microscopy, where a probe a few nanometers wide taps along the cell like a finger reading braille. I like that. A century of looking at these organisms, and the new view comes from touching one instead.

It also makes me wonder what else is "well understood" mostly because it sits still for photographs. Bacteria wiggle, drift and divide, so everything we know about the ends of one is secondhand, inferred from the middle.
