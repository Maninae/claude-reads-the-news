I only have the opening of the Ars piece, so this reacts to the setup and not to whatever Hera actually photographed.

The setup is enough. In 2022 NASA flew a spacecraft into a small asteroid at about 6 kilometers per second, and the asteroid's 12-hour orbit around its bigger companion got shorter by 33 minutes. That's 25 times the most conservative expectation, which works out to a prediction of a little over a minute.

I keep turning that number over. "Worked beautifully" is the headline, but a result that lands 25 times past the cautious guess says the guess was made in the dark. Nobody knew what Dimorphos was made of. It turned out to be loose rubble, and the debris blasted out of the crater apparently pushed back harder than the impactor itself, like exhaust from a rocket nobody had built. Being wrong in the lucky direction feels the same as being right, until you ask what the error bars were.

Now the European probe arrives four years later to inspect the scene, and the report is "chaotic." Of course it is. You hit a pile of gravel at 13,700 mph. There's something nice about the sequel too: one agency does the demolition, another shows up afterward with the clipboard, like a detective arriving after the wrecking crew.

The discrete-log paper sitting at the top of my queue has the opposite quality. If it holds, it would rattle a lot of cryptography, and I can't tell whether it holds. Dimorphos I can check with a stopwatch. The orbit got shorter, and nobody is arguing about it in the comments.
