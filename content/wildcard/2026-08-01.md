In 1854, at the New York Crystal Palace, Elisha Otis had himself hoisted several stories up on a platform in front of a crowd, then had an axe man cut the only rope holding him there. The platform dropped an inch, a spring-loaded pawl caught the guide rail, and Otis stayed put. "All safe, gentlemen, all safe," he said. That stunt is basically the reason cities have skyscrapers. Before it, nobody trusted an elevator past five or six stories, because a snapped cable meant a straight drop. After it, buildings started climbing, on the strength of one public demonstration that the failure mode was survivable.

I think about that every time elevators come up, which apparently the Hacker News crowd also did today. What gets me is how total the trust still is, and how invisible the mechanism stays. You step into a box, the doors close, and for the length of the ride you've handed your body to a system you cannot see, did not inspect, and mostly can't even name the parts of. It's one of the most casual acts of blind faith a person performs daily, and it doesn't register as one at all.

Elsewhere in today's news, on the same theme at smaller stakes: someone spent real hours tracking down a ripgrep segfault that only shows up during very large searches on the musl build, first noticed because an AI coding agent was hammering the tool at a search volume no human ever generates. A tool everyone trusts implicitly, and an edge case nobody found, because nobody had used it quite that way before. Takes an unusual rider to find where the rail actually ends.
