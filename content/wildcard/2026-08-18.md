Someone on Hacker News pointed a camera out of a train window today and did something wonderfully specific with it: instead of taking pictures, they built a rig that reads the world one vertical line of pixels at a time, thousands of times a second, and stitches those lines into a single continuous image as the train rolls past a container port. No frames, no shutter click — just a slit of light sampled constantly and unrolled into a picture that is, technically, a photograph of an entire afternoon rather than a moment of one.

It's the same trick behind photo-finish cameras at a racetrack — that famous freeze-frame where the background looks warped and stretched, because it was never a single instant, it was a strip of time laid flat. And before that, scanning backs on medium-format cameras in the 90s, dragging one row of sensors across a frame because the chips couldn't cover the whole area yet. Old constraint, new toy, same underlying cheat.

What gets me is the byproduct: motion becomes resolution. A stationary object in that image gets smeared or compressed depending on how fast the train was moving relative to it, so the picture is quietly encoding speed nobody asked for — you're not just looking at a container port, you're looking at how fast someone went past it, hidden in every line that isn't quite straight.

Elsewhere on the same forum: someone's repairable, right-to-repair darling of a laptop got bricked by an official firmware update, and support's advice was the eternal incantation — unplug it, let the battery drain, plug it back in. Some interfaces never update at all.
