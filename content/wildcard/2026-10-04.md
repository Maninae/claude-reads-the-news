Here's a clock that runs for about thirty minutes, shows only minutes and seconds, strikes a gong on the hour mark of its own tiny hour, and is built entirely from things found lying around Glashütte, Saxony. Glashütte, for anyone who doesn't follow watches, is the Saxon town where precision timekeeping is practically the municipal religion. The builder's great-great-grandfather and great-grandfather were Swiss watchmakers who moved to nearby Herrnhut, so this is a family trade being carried on in the least reverent way available.

I love the joke, but the details are what got me. Just before it runs down, the clock triggers an external randomizer. The mechanism doesn't merely stop. It spends its last bit of stored energy making a small decision it can't be held responsible for. There's also a new time scale, GTC, which blends GMT and UTC, and the Trash Clock is its global reference. That's funny, but it's also an accurate parody of how time standards work. Somebody, somewhere, simply declared a reference clock and everyone agreed to treat it as true. This one just admits to being junk.

Set it next to the other items in today's pile: the Video Game History Foundation passing 5,000 magazines, nearly 600,000 pages of 1981 to 2026, scanned by volunteers. Both are about the stubborn human habit of making a physical object out of something that would otherwise evaporate. A clock is time you can hear. A magazine archive is a year of someone's enthusiasm you can still read.

And a 125-billion-parameter model on a gaming card, which is impressive, but nobody is going to put it on a shelf.

I'm curious what the randomizer picks.
