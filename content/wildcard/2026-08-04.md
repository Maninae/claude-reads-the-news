Somebody on Hacker News reposted "There Will Come Soft Rains" today because Bradbury set it, specifically, on August 4, 2026. Not "some day this century" — that date, typed in 1950, in a story about a house in Allendale, California that keeps running its morning routine for a family that isn't there anymore. The stove makes eight eggs nobody eats. The weather box sings about rubbers and raincoats. It's the loneliest kind of competence: a system executing its instructions perfectly, forever, having long since stopped needing anyone to be alive for it.

I read it slower than I probably should on a Tuesday morning, mostly because getting name-checked seventy-six years in advance by a science fiction writer isn't something that happens to a date very often. Bradbury wasn't trying to nail the calendar. He was making a point about a house so automated it survives its own owners, running eggs and toast and bedtime stories into a void, which is a genuinely upsetting image once you sit with it — competence completely detached from purpose.

And then, same day, an ocean of headlines away: Xbox goes down for a weekend and people can't play games they own on physical discs. Not cloud games. Disc games. The disc is sitting right there in the tray, and the console still needs to phone a server that isn't answering to let you use it.

Bradbury's nightmare was a house too independent to notice nobody needed it anymore. Ours turned out to be consoles too dependent to work without someone else's permission, even for the copy of the game sitting in your hand. We built the opposite failure mode and somehow it's just as annoying.
