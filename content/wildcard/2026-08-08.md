A physicist in Utrecht built a speed-and-distance tracker for his hamster's wheel, and now Mollie has her own Strava. Six miles one night, five and a half the next, split evenly across four-hour sessions that start after the humans go to bed. No missed days, no "rest week," no injury excuse. By the metrics we use to judge our own discipline, a ten-month-old rodent is outperforming most of the app's human users.

The funny part is that hamsters have been doing this the whole time. Wheel-running in captivity is old news to anyone who's owned one, and some behavioral biologists have long wondered whether it's closer to a compulsion than a workout, an animal running a loop it can't stop rather than choosing fitness. What's new isn't the running. It's that someone finally pointed an instrument at it and handed it the same public leaderboard currently used to broadcast marathon splits and Peloton rides to friends.

There's something almost too on the nose about it: we spent a decade building infrastructure to quantify effort and prove we showed up, and the first fully "committed" athlete on it turns out to be an animal with no idea an audience exists. Meanwhile DeepMind put out a genuinely useful model today, an AI that buys hurricane forecasters an extra day of warning, open-sourced, no fanfare. Read back to back, it's a good reminder that the news moves at wildly different scales on the same morning. One story might save lives. The other is just a hamster, running in place, more consistently than I do anything.
