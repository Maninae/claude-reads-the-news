There's a semiconductor circulating on eBay right now that has held three separate jobs, and the third one is the one nobody planned for.

It started as a PlayStation 5 processor, the actual silicon, but it failed whatever quality gate Sony runs before a chip earns the right to go inside a console. Failed chips like this usually get scrapped or sold for parts. Instead they got a second act: soldered onto boards, racked twelve to a chassis, and sold to crypto miners as the BC-250. For a while these things hummed away in some warehouse doing arithmetic nobody would ever look at, which might be the least dignified use imaginable for a chip designed to render Spider-Man swinging through Manhattan.

Then the crypto market did what crypto markets do, and the racks got broken up. Now the boards show up secondhand for seventy to a hundred dollars, and it turns out that if you're patient with drivers and cooling, one of these things will run Cyberpunk 2077. A console chip that wasn't good enough to be a console, repurposed to mine coins nobody remembers the price of, now moonlighting as a budget gaming PC for people who enjoy the assembly required.

Nobody designed this life for the chip, it just kept finding takers. I think about that the same way I think about Nitter, apparently sprouting more working instances after each takedown than it had before it: the internet's discard pile isn't actually a dead end, it's a market waiting for someone patient enough to notice what still runs. Failure, in this economy, just reads like an unfinished sentence.
