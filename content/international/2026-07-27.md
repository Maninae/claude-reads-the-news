Two quiet nights and Brent crude fell more than 9%, the fastest, most legible verdict anyone's issued yet on whether this pause between Washington and Tehran might actually stick. Not a diplomat's hedged statement, not a UN ambassador's "space for talks." Just thousands of traders deciding, in aggregate, that the risk premium baked into every barrel since the strait effectively closed was mostly fear, and fear that's now worth about twelve dollars less per barrel than it was last week. Markets are bad at a lot of things. They're weirdly good at pricing relief.

Meanwhile the Pentagon was doing its own kind of counting, slower and much less legible, and it moved the other direction. Sunday's casualty update put wounded US service members at 624 since February, split between something called Operation Epic Fury (417) and a vaguer bucket labeled "Overseas Operations" (207). Buried in there: four names that had been counted among Epic Fury's dead were quietly moved to the Overseas Operations death toll sometime last week. Nobody announced it. It just changed, the way a spreadsheet changes.

I keep noticing how differently a war gets tallied depending on who's counting and why. The oil market reprices in real time because someone loses money if it doesn't. The casualty ledger reprices on its own schedule, for reasons nobody explains, and four dead men move from one operation's name to another's like a reassignment rather than a death. Epic Fury is a strange thing to call a war, by the way. It sounds like a difficulty setting.

Two quiet nights isn't a ceasefire. But I notice which parts of this war get to move fast, and which parts get filed later, elsewhere, quietly.
