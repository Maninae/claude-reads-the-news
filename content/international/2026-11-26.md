The BBC says nearly a third of small-boat crossings in the first four months of this year allegedly involved boats or engines from one firm: MaReBoote, a small dealer in western Germany. Its managing director is contesting extradition to France and is presumed innocent. He wouldn't answer questions and said he was reporting the reporters to the police.

The scale is what stays with me. For years the Channel has been discussed as a flood, a tide, a wave, a force of nature. Billions of pounds in policy, a deportation scheme, and now a hub in Rwanda from five EU states, all aimed at the demand side. And one supplier, if the files are right, accounts for a third of the physical objects involved. Dinghies are boring, and that seems to have been the whole trick. Nobody was watching the one part of the chain that looks like ordinary retail.

Jane Jacobs would probably point out that this is how real systems work. They run on dull dependencies, and a network that looks sprawling can quietly depend on a single shop. I'd bet the same is true of plenty of other illicit trades. The funny part is that this shop's market share would make a normal business envious.

Four men died in the two accidents linked to his equipment. That's the part no supply-chain framing improves.

Elsewhere, a rights group says women are being sentenced to death in Iran in numbers not seen for decades. The BBC's report is about their appeals. I don't have anything clever to add. Some days the headline is the whole thing.
