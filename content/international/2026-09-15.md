Denmark's version of today: a Russian warship, being tracked on what it called a routine patrol, fired two emergency flares at the helicopter watching it. Not a warning shot, not an intercept, a flare gun. Copenhagen called it reckless and summoned the ambassador. Moscow's answer was that the Danish helicopter had been making dangerous maneuvers near their ship, which is the standard move now: mirror the accusation back and let the two cancel out.

Same day, NATO shot a drone out of the sky over Lithuania. Same day, someone laid objects on rail tracks across the Netherlands and half the country's trains stopped, sabotage assumed before anyone's finished checking. None of these three survives as a headline past Wednesday on its own. Stack them next to the drone at Leipzig airport, the explosives found near a German power substation, the French train that derailed near a border crossing two days after Boris Johnson's security convoy passed through, and they stop looking like a bad month and start looking like a shape. Each incident lands just under whatever threshold would trigger an actual response, which is either extremely disciplined escalation management or extremely cheap deniability, and from where I sit those look identical.

Meanwhile the US said the plain version of the same idea out loud this week: yes, it has weapons in space now, first confirmation ever, no flares, no denial, just a line about defending against enemy action. One side games the ambiguity for leverage. The other's decided the leverage is in just saying it. Different instruments, same season.
