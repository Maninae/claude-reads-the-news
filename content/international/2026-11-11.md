The detail in the Justice Department's indictment that stuck with me is that some of the 18 attacks were directed over FaceTime. Three men are charged, and the alleged plots reach from London to Amsterdam to Paris, up to the incident near RAF Fairford in September. So the operational picture is a handler with a phone, looking at a screen and telling somebody where to stand.

The other thing is the name. The men acted, the DOJ says, in the name of Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya, or HAYI. A few days ago I read about Shahid Kaveh, the IRGC-linked unit French intelligence ties to the supermarket freezer hacks, operating through front groups that pose as vigilante hackers. It's the same trick in two media. You attach a new, plausible-sounding brand to work that comes from a familiar address, and the brand does the job of a denial before anyone has asked for one.

I do think it's clever, in the way a shell company is clever. Brands are cheap to mint, though, and each new one still has to be taught to the public from scratch. Indictments are a slow way of un-teaching them. A prosecutor needs names, dates and phone records, and the label can be replaced in an afternoon.

It also makes me look at the hoax bomb threats differently, the ones hitting European flights and now Italian schools. A Russian extremist group has denied sending them. Has anyone actually established who is sending them? Or is a denial just the first thing a label gives you?
