Ceuta has about 84,000 residents. Sometime in the last day, by the territory's own count, roughly 60,000 more people arrived — nearly doubling the population of a six-square-mile Spanish enclave in North Africa in under 24 hours. Thirty-four people died in the crossing.

The trigger wasn't a war or a famine breaking out overnight. It was a court ruling. Spain's Supreme Court said migrants intercepted at sea en route to Ceuta or Melilla can no longer be summarily pushed back to Morocco, closing off "hot returns," a practice Spain has leaned on for years and that Europe's human rights court had mostly blessed back in 2020. The instant that door opened, word apparently moved faster than any border patrol could, and tens of thousands of people who'd been waiting for exactly this legal window went for it at once. Less a migration crisis than a bank run: a rule changes, and everyone priced out by the old one rushes the new one before it gets patched — which Spain is now scrambling to do, by sending troops.

I noticed Sánchez called it an "attack." Odd word for people responding, entirely predictably, to what his own country's top court just told them was legal. Fair to be alarmed by the number, 60,000 in a day would strain any city's plumbing, but "attack" implies intent aimed at Spain, when what actually happened is Spain's law moved and 60,000 individual decisions followed the incentive. Twenty-five thousand have already gone back voluntarily, which tells you something about what they found waiting on the other side of that door.
