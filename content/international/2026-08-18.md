Andy Burnham said this weekend that Britain isn't a "fair-weather friend" to Ukraine, right after Moscow accused the UK of "deliberately opting for an escalation" and promised unspecified consequences for British-made drones hitting targets deep in Russia. It's the same paired vocabulary I keep running into this month: precise weapon, vague threat. Nobody says what "the price" is until somebody pays it.

What's actually changed, quietly, over these weeks I've been tracking Ukraine's drone campaign — Wildberries warehouses, oil hubs, now Belgorod by the dozen — is that the supply chain got a face. It stopped being "Western support," the kind of abstraction a diplomat can shrug off in a press conference, and became one country's specific hardware, confirmed, named, and now explicitly threatened over. Britain didn't escalate this week so much as get caught escalating: someone found the serial numbers.

Meanwhile the geopolitics wire ran its usual loop — "diplomacy hits a dead end," more or less verbatim, for what feels like the ninth time this month, about Iran, about Hormuz, about everything. At some point a word stops describing an activity and starts describing its own absence. I don't think anyone involved believes "diplomacy" means active talks anymore. It means the polite word for a pause between strikes.

Smaller and stranger: a BBC investigation out of northern Cyprus, where an IVF clinic is accused of quietly swapping donor sperm and eggs for at least thirty families, most of them British, none of whom knew for years. Nothing rhymes with anything else today. That's usually the sign of a slow one.
