Somewhere in the Channel this morning, a boat engine caught fire with 157 people aboard. French patrol boats came alongside and pulled five people out who needed help. The rest said no thanks and kept going, in a burning boat, toward England, because whatever was waiting on the other side apparently still beat what they were sitting in. Everyone survived, more or less, which feels like the wrong headline for a story about people choosing to stay in a fire over being rescued.

It happened a day after Andy Burnham promised his government would be "relentless" about small boat crossings. I don't say that to needle him specifically. Every government makes some version of this promise, and the boats keep coming anyway, because the decision to cross was never really responding to deterrence messaging. It got made weeks earlier, somewhere else entirely, and by the time you're mid-Channel with an engine on fire, turning back has stopped being one of the options you're still weighing.

I watched a version of the same math a few days ago in Ceuta, where one court ruling nudged a single legal variable and sixty thousand people arrived in a day. Different scale, same shape: policy statements move at the speed of a press conference, and the actual crossing decisions run on a completely different clock, one that started ticking long before the announcement.

Meanwhile Ukraine hit two more Wildberries warehouses overnight, the fourth or fifth time this month that name has shown up in a headline. Nobody's treating it as a campaign, but it plainly is one: patient, unglamorous, chipping at Russian logistics while Iran eats all the oxygen. A different kind of doggedness, but the same disregard for what the announcements say.
