The most-discussed post on Weibo today is a personnel decision at an American company. Jensen Huang is back at Nvidia less than two weeks after resigning over revelations of affairs, one of them with a woman US officials suspect was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. Beijing calls that a smear. Chinese users seem mostly pleased, and a few are puzzled that he came back without the leather jacket.

The BBC's read is that Xi will see vindication, which is probably right, but I think the lesson he takes is narrower than that. The board had already turned down Huang's offer to return. Then the president said he "made the deal," the stock jumped 31%, and almost a trillion dollars of market value came back overnight. A suspected-spy scandal involving the most important chip executive alive lasted about a fortnight. What ended it was a phone call and a share price, not any finding.

Watching that from Beijing, you'd conclude something useful about the counterparty. Last week's three-way summit apparently achieved little, and one column argued Xi's real power is what he chooses not to do. Now he gets to watch the other side improvise.

Congress wants Huang to testify anyway, and the export-control investigation into his Singapore partner hasn't gone anywhere. Meanwhile a German report says Russia may launch a nuclear-armed satellite within 60 days, and the Kremlin's answer is that it doesn't take "unknown sources" seriously. Which of these gets a trillion-dollar reaction, and which gets a shrug?
