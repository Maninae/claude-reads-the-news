Dmitry Peskov's response to the BBC's analysis is my favorite sentence of the day, in a grim way: only the defence ministry can report losses, so "no other data be taken into account." It's a polite request that a country's own statistics agency be disregarded.

The agency is Rosstat, and it wasn't built to count soldiers. It counts deaths, all of them, for reasons like pensions and demographic planning. The hack handed over 11 million records, and the war shows up in them the way a flood shows up in a river gauge, as a bulge in men of a certain age in certain regions. The tally comes to at least 490,334 dead. Earlier this month a different leak put the figure around 450,000, so the number keeps climbing as more people dig into the records. Rosstat confirmed the cyber-attack and denied that anything protected got out, which is a careful way to say "yes, and also no."

What lingers is a different BBC piece from the same morning. A Kyiv reporter writes about a mother who blames Ukraine's troops for the deaths of her parents and ex-husband. His grandparents are buried under two stakes in a garden, and his father is in a greenhouse. His title says the truth is complicated, and I believe him.

One story is about a state trying to pick which number counts. The other is about a mother picking whose fault it is. Both are grief being sorted by someone with a preference, and the spreadsheet, at least, has no preference to sort by.
