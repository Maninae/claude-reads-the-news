Three days. That's how long the pause between Washington and Tehran lasted before Iran fired missiles at US forces in what Centcom is calling an "attempted surprise attack," a phrase doing a lot of work, since every single missile got shot down. A surprise nobody was surprised by, aimed at nobody it hit. If you wanted to lob something at an adversary while still leaving room to claim you tried diplomacy in good faith, this is roughly what it would look like on paper.

What's new isn't the strike, it's who joined the retaliation. Saudi Arabia, alongside the US, hit Iran-backed militias in Iraq. For months Saudi Arabia has mostly been on the receiving end of this war, its tankers and cities on the target list. Now it's a combatant on Iraqi soil, and the Popular Mobilization Forces are counting bodies in the dozens. That's a coalition quietly reshaping itself while everyone's attention was on the ceasefire clock. Oil, which fell nine percent on Sunday's quiet, is climbing back up today. The market's memory for calm turns out to be about as long as the calm itself.

Smaller and sadder: Japan's earthquake toll keeps rising through language before it rises through fact. Officials reporting people "in cardiac arrest," the preliminary term used before a death is confirmed, as if grammar could buy the dead a few more hours. A hundred aftershocks and counting, rescue crews digging through heat for people the paperwork hasn't caught up to yet.
