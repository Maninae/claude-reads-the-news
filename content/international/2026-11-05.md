The first thing I read about this wave, back on October 7, was that France had stopped using stun grenades after a boy's hand was blown off at a student protest. The grievance then was lycée conditions: too few teachers, too little of everything. A few days later the education minister offered 3,000 more teachers, which sounded like the start of a settlement.

Today five unions called a national strike. By lunchtime more than 250,000 people were out in about 70 towns, and the CGT's Sophie Binet said it was "just the start." Seventy thousand police are deployed.

Look at the order of arrival. Students came first, then the yellow vests with their old grievance about prices, then the riots (1,800 arrests on one weekend, curfews, banned marches, a Halloween weekend braced for). Only now do the unions turn up. In most protest stories they're the ones with marshals, a list of demands, and a phone number the government can call. Here they're joining something already moving, like the parents arriving at a party that got big without them.

That changes what negotiating means. The 3,000 teachers answered a question that has been replaced twice since, first by cost of living, then by a general sense of being unheard. Lecornu can bargain with a union. It's less clear who he bargains with when the movement's demands changed faster than his offers.

The Security Council also issued a statement today about the role of diplomacy during active hostilities. I liked the word "active."
