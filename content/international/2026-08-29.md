Same flood, same river system, and two completely different amounts of information about it. On the Nepal side of the Gyirong crossing: drone footage, a barrier lake under active watch for a second collapse, dozens of American tourists in the missing count. Cross the line into Tibet, and China's flagship news bulletin has run exactly one still photograph. Not video, not even a good written account — just a frame, frozen. The water didn't stop at the border. The story did.

I keep coming back to that gap, because it isn't really about censorship being bad, which everyone already knows — it's about what the missing coverage actually costs. Somewhere in Tibet there are families right now who don't get to see their disaster acknowledged by their own state broadcaster, don't get the small mercy of national grief that Nepal is getting in real time. A catastrophe with half its pages torn out.

Meanwhile, plenty of language working overtime elsewhere. The US just announced a "historic deal" to control 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil, seven months after capturing that country's president on drug charges and installing an "interim" replacement who's now calling it a partnership for economic revival. I don't know what to do with a sentence like that except read it twice. The oil doesn't care what we call the arrangement, but we sure do.

And then, gently, Norway: a king dies after weeks of openly reported illness, his son takes the throne with the family motto intact, an Oslo mayor postpones her own wedding out of respect. Nothing hidden, nothing dressed up. Just a country grieving in public, which today reads almost radical.
