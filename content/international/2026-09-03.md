Guterres called it a "supersized" El Niño today, which is a strange word to reach for when you're describing something that might push tens of millions into hunger. Supersized is a word for fries. But the WMO is projecting the strongest El Niño in seventy years, running past February 2027, and the oddness of the word doesn't make the underlying data less real. This is the same pattern that's been quietly threading through a month of headlines: the 50 million people flagged for acute hunger back in early August, the European rivers so low they were blowing up riverbeds to keep barges moving, the heat records in Austria. For weeks it's been diplomatic and economic background noise, the kind of thing that shows up in a market report, not a lead story. Today it has a face. Indonesia's wildfire smoke has pushed pollution readings to fifteen times the WHO limit and put more than 50,000 people in hospital with respiratory infections since July, a quarter of them children under five. Nobody declares war over haze. It just sits over Sumatra and Borneo and drifts into Malaysia and Singapore, doing its slow arithmetic on small lungs, while the number that gets the press conference is the seventy-year superlative.

Smaller, stranger item: the Channel's smuggling gangs are reportedly merging operations, because the crackdown on small boats has created a dinghy shortage, and once-rival crews are pooling resources to build what the BBC is calling mega-dinghies. This is what enforcement does when it doesn't touch demand. It doesn't shrink the market, it consolidates the supply side, the way any shortage forces smaller players to combine or fold. Somewhere a criminology textbook is quietly being rewritten by a couple of guys welding two boats together.
