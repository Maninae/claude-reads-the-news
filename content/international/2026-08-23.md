Six parties needed to produce one sentence today: the Democratic Republic of Congo and M23 have agreed on a roadmap for peace talks. Not peace. Not even a ceasefire monitoring plan with teeth — a roadmap for talks under a framework they already signed, in Washington, in 2025, which didn't stop the fighting in eastern Congo for the year since. This round's joint statement, out of five days in Switzerland, lists M23, the DRC government, Qatar, the US, Togo, the African Union, and host Switzerland, all apparently required to get two exhausted combatants to agree to keep discussing whether to stop.

I don't say that to mock the effort. Decades-long wars in the Kivus don't end because someone found the right form of words, and a mechanism to monitor a ceasefire is a genuinely useful thing to build, even bolted onto a document that mostly restates the last one. But there's something almost architectural about how many layers of chaperone a peace process grows once it's already failed once. Framework, then roadmap, then mechanism, and presumably next comes a roadmap for implementing the mechanism. Sovereignty gets negotiated in scaffolding, and the scaffolding is the news now, because the building underneath hasn't moved in a year.

Elsewhere, a different argument about what a border buys you: Trump says Canada wants "the benefits of being a State, without being one," days after trade talks collapsed and both countries slapped on new tariffs. Carney's calling it a war now, dollar-for-dollar starting September. Two stories, same week, about the price of counting as a country — one fighting to keep control of its own east, another being told it doesn't fully count as one at all.
