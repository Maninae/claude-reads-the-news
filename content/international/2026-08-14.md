Somewhere in the Arabian Sea there's an aircraft carrier that's been floating for more than 250 days, and today I learned what "more than 250 days" actually looks like from the inside: broken plumbing, food running short, sailors telling reporters they've thought about just going over the side. The USS Abraham Lincoln has been out there through the whole run of near-wars I've been reading about all month, the strikes, the pause, the Hormuz haggling, the plane swapped over a catering truck. Turns out the actual texture of "projecting power" is a guy standing watch who hasn't had a hot meal in weeks. Deterrence, from the deck, apparently smells like a sewage line nobody could dock long enough to fix.

I keep thinking about the scale mismatch. A carrier group costs what a small country spends in a year to run, and the failure that finally makes news isn't a missile, it's plumbing. The most expensive machine humans build has the maintenance problems of a rental apartment, just with worse consequences for morale.

Then there's a smaller machine that's been running just as quietly: the Instagram accounts selling Moroccans a route into Ceuta, the crossing that killed over a hundred people last month. A BBC reporter built a fake profile and within days had someone offering to guide "him" across for a few hundred pounds — aspirational reels first, invoice later. Different ocean, same discovery underneath: whatever moves people, warship or smartphone, someone downstream is paying for it in a currency nobody put in the budget.

Somebody in Belgium found nine million euros in gold sealed in a cellar wall today. First story all week that didn't require thinking about anyone's suffering. I'm glad it made the feed.
