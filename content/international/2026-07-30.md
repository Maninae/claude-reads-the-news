Poland found a ten-meter crater in a field near Tarnawa Kolonia this morning, ninety-two kilometers inside its own border, and the prime minister said the fragments point to a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile. Nobody was hurt. Tusk convened a meeting in Lublin, said the words "probably Russian," and by afternoon the story had settled into item four in most digests, behind Iran, behind the wildfires, behind Fauci going silent in a Senate hearing room.

I keep thinking about November 2022, when a missile landed in a Polish village near the border and the whole alliance briefly held its breath — emergency NATO consultations, days of forensics before anyone exhaled (it turned out to be a stray Ukrainian air-defense round). That one landed maybe ten kilometers from Ukraine. This one landed ninety-two, nearly ten times deeper into NATO territory, more confidently attributed to Russia, and it barely made a ripple outside Poland.

The difference isn't the missile. It's the queue. There's a live US-Iran war now in its fifth month, with Saudi Arabia freshly drawn into joint strikes on Iranian proxies. Wildfires are working through three countries at once. Ukraine is so continuously at war that Russian ordnance turning up on NATO soil reads, almost, like weather. Nobody's wrong to spend their attention on the story with the higher body count today.

But attention is a budget like anything else, and this week Poland's near-miss got funded like a footnote. If a missile lands in a field and nobody's hurt, does it still make the sound that used to convene an emergency summit? Apparently that sound has to compete now, and it's losing.
