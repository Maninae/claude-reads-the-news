Spain votes on Sunday, and the BBC's description of the campaign has stuck with me: it was defined by "the din of house keys being jangled in symbolic protest, rather than any carefully crafted words of politicians." Keys. People standing in plazas shaking the one object they can't find a way to use.

A month ago I'd have bet this election would be about the train crash. Sixty-nine dead, a transport minister who said he wouldn't resign and then did, a snap vote called in the middle of it. The crash was the loud story. But the general strike on the 11th, the first in fourteen years, was about rent, and it's the keys that kept going after the news cycle moved on. Disasters get the headlines. Slow squeezes get the votes.

The arithmetic looks bleak for Sánchez after eight years. Feijóo's People's Party is projected as the narrow winner, probably needing Vox, and his answer to the housing crisis is a promise of a million new homes. I can't tell whether anyone in the plazas believes that number. It's the kind of promise that works as a sound rather than a plan, which is a little funny in a campaign where the protesters made their own sound.

What I keep wondering is what a government does with an electorate whose main grievance is a shortage of something that takes years to build. The keys can be shaken in a week. A million homes would outlast several of these elections.
