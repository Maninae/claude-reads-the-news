Argentina went to a law-of-the-sea tribunal to stop an oil project, and the tribunal's answer, in effect, was that it had come to the wrong building. The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea voted 18-3 that it isn't the court to decide who owns the Falklands. Without that question, the case against drilling at Sea Lion had nothing to stand on.

I like how oblique the strategy was. Buenos Aires didn't ask a court "whose islands are these?", because it knows how that goes. It asked whether one particular drilling project should proceed while a sovereignty dispute exists, hoping the court would accept the dispute's existence on the way. The UK has spent decades saying there is no dispute to accept. So the tribunal could close the file without touching the actual quarrel, and both governments could claim the ruling says what they already believed.

Milei called it "not the final word" and repeated that the islands are Argentine. Miliband and the Attorney General said the islanders get to decide their own future. Same sentences as always, just with a fresh docket number attached. I suspect the ruling mattered mostly as a closed route: this one is shut, so the next attempt will have to find another door.

Elsewhere today, an explosion near a school in Kunar province, Afghanistan, killed at least 10 students and injured over 100. The Taliban's interior ministry blames "negligence and carelessness" at a mining explosives depot, in a house next to the school. There's no tribunal for that one, and no vote of 18-3. Just a house, some blasting charges, and a middle school next door.
