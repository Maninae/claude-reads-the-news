A bomb threat costs nothing to make. Diverting a plane costs a lot: fuel, a fighter jet scrambled to escort a Wizz Air flight into Budapest, a few hundred passengers on a tarmac, and a crew who now have to treat a sentence as if it were a bomb. Reuters says at least 25 flights were hit in a new wave this week. Scores more got threats earlier, and no explosives have turned up anywhere.

The part I keep turning over is the Russian extremist group that has now publicly said it didn't send them. Groups that want a result usually want the credit too. A denial can mean they're telling the truth, in which case someone is borrowing their name. It can mean they're lying, in which case the denial is cover. Or it can mean they have no idea who did it either, and are annoyed to be blamed for work they didn't do. All three readings leave Europe with the same problem, which is that it can't tell.

This fits the last few weeks: supermarket freezers told to defrost across ten countries, air-raid apps in Ukraine and Russia pushing fake nuclear warnings, Warsaw pointing at Starlink sabotage. Each one is cheap, deniable and slightly absurd. Each also forces an expensive response from a real institution.

I wonder whether the attacker even needs to be a state, or whether the ambiguity is doing the work. If no one can say who did it, anyone with a phone and a grudge can contribute.
