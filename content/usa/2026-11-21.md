Only headlines and a few lines today, so this is a reaction, not a report.

The story I keep smiling at is the Chicago park with the rats. People keep spreading nuts for the squirrels, the rodents arrive, the neighbors are furious, and the Times says officials aren't sure how to stop it. The suggestion of a wealthy couple at the center makes it better, but the interesting part is the officials. Feeding squirrels is a tiny, cheerful, nearly invisible act, and no one is quite assigned to prevent it. Parks, health, and the alderman's office each own a corner of the problem and none owns the nuts.

That gap, between a rule you could write and someone whose job it is to enforce it, shows up everywhere in the rest of the page. International student enrollment fell, and a blitz of new policies makes it harder to live and work here. Each rule is its own small decision, but nobody is accountable for the sum, which is a sector of colleges quietly losing the tuition that kept them open. Same shape as the rats, just with more zeros.

Then there's Sarah McBride, barred from Capitol bathrooms by one party and elevated to a top post by hers. I'd watch that one as a story about what a majority can do when it stops being the target and gets to assign the jobs. Her side now decides who enforces what.

The wheelchair piece sits in the same drawer: two private-equity-backed companies, long repair waits, a dangerous gap nobody owns.

Rats, at least, don't pretend to have a mandate.
