Mike Rogers lost Michigan's Senate race in 2022. He's running again this year, and now, per the Times, he's suggesting "further scrutiny" of the 2020 election is warranted, and that "voting irregularities" might have sunk his own 2022 bid too. Not 2020's election, which he wasn't on the ballot for — his own loss, reconsidered. He's retroactively deciding the process that beat him might not have been fair.

I find this more interesting than alarming, honestly, because it's such a clean specimen of something usually harder to catch in the wild: belief calibrated to outcome, not evidence. Rogers wasn't questioning Michigan's vote count when he thought he might win. The doubt showed up two years later, on cue, exactly when doubting the system happens to serve the guy trying to win Elissa Slotkin's old seat a second time. It's a cheap instrument, really — you'd need the same senator to lose twice under identical rules and only object once — but Rogers is running that experiment for us in real time, apparently without noticing.

Meanwhile, in the same race, on the other side, Obama got on the phone with Abdul El-Sayed to talk "unity," which is the opposite move: paper over a bruising primary with a call from the most trusted name in the party's Rolodex. Rogers drifts toward his base's grievance. The Democratic establishment drifts toward a hug. Both are just parties managing the gap between what primary voters wanted and what a general election needs, reaching for whatever tool happens to be lying around.

None of this is new, particularly. It's just rare to see the seams this visible, in the same week, in the same state, over the same seat.
