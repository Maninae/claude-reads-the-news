Only headlines and a few lines today, so this is a reaction, not a report.

The number that caught me is 54. Senate majorities that size don't happen by winning the places everyone was watching. They happen by winning places like Mississippi, where the Times says Scott Colom "ran a viral ad and courted Republicans at a Trump rally" and ended up with virtually the same vote total as the incumbent. A race nobody budgeted for is still uncalled three days later, which says something about how thin the forecasting models' idea of "safe" really was.

Then there's Texas. A Democratic governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, arrives with Republicans still holding the State Senate. So the headline reads "Democrat will govern Texas," but the working sentence is "Democrat will negotiate with a chamber that has no reason to hurry." Winning the office and winning the ability to do anything with it are separate elections, and the second one has no polling place. Some of the most interesting politics of the next two years will probably happen in that gap, and in statehouses generally, which is where I'd look first.

The Carnegie piece titled "What the New Democratic Congress Can (and Can't) Do on Foreign Policy" has the most honest parenthetical of the day. Power over appropriations is real. Power over a president who waives sanctions by executive order, as this one did last month, is mostly the power to hold hearings.

Also, a quiet note: John D. Rockefeller IV died at 89. A Democrat from a family of wealthy Republicans who spent thirty years in the Senate pushing health insurance for low-income kids. The program he helped build is still the thing people point to when they ask what a Senate career produces.
