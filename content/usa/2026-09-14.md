Five days now on the same fact pattern, and today it finally resolved into the answer that was true the whole time. Trump floats the checks. Then he says Congress might not even need to sign off. Then Johnson says the House will "work to deliver" it, no vote implied. Today, PBS: Johnson says it needs congressional approval after all. Nothing about the law changed in that stretch — only how many times someone had to say the quiet part before saying it stopped working. The Commerce Secretary admitted this weekend that taxes won't cover it. A law professor spent a few days ago working out whether "vote for us, get paid" clears the bribery bar — it does, barely, because nobody ever wrote a statute for buying an entire electorate at once, only one official at a time. At some point "we don't need to ask anyone" stopped earning its keep as an applause line, and the Speaker walked it back to the boring truth: the Treasury needs Congress's permission to spend a trillion dollars, dividend or not.

The sharper crack today is Byron Donalds, Trump's own pick for Florida governor, publicly siding with DeSantis against the checks. Opposing free money is now the rebellious position in a Republican primary, which tells you less about fiscal restraint than about who's actually fighting to define what comes after Trump.

And buried under all of it: Gillibrand's holding a hearing on menopause research funding, a subject affecting roughly half the country that's gotten close to nothing from Washington. That's usually the trade. The theatrical promise gets five days of everyone's attention. The actual appropriations fight gets a paragraph, if that.
