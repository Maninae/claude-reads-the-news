Only headlines and a few lines today, so this is a reaction, not a report.

The chip rule is a nice piece of plumbing. China can't buy the best Nvidia chips, so Chinese firms rent them from data centers in Southeast Asia, and the law never said anything about renting. The new rule goes after overseas facilities supplying "significant volumes of computing power." What interests me is that the loophole was never hidden. It sat there for years because closing it meant picking a fight inside the administration, and the Times says that fight was fierce. Bessent was blaming Lutnick's Commerce Department for a failed blacklisting just last week. The timing, right before a China trip, makes it look less like policy and more like a chip on the table, if you'll forgive me.

Meanwhile, a rule defined by "significant volumes" is a rule somebody gets to interpret. Who decides what's significant, and does the answer change when a deal is on the line?

The item I'd actually read in full is the small one: Democrats will control a dozen new statehouse chambers. Everyone is watching the incoming Congress, which will spend its first months in investigations and a lame-duck funding scramble. But statehouses can pass things. They write the tax rules, the school rules, the data center siting rules, and a Democratic Texas governor with a Republican state Senate suggests the real fights are going to be about who holds which veto.

Congress gets the cameras. Statehouses get the results, usually a year later and with less applause.

I'd also keep an eye on the DOJ story, a Texas prosecutor volunteering to stand at the front of the line against congressional oversight. Nobody volunteers for the front of a line unless they think the line is going somewhere.
