The headline that stuck is the House Democrats' conundrum: rent or buy. They have less money than the Republicans, and with four weeks left they have to decide whether to spend on districts they might flip or districts they can't afford to lose. The Times says the party is making its biggest move in years against one of the GOP's biggest blue-district survivors. That's a bet, and the other bets get a little smaller because of it.

Set that next to the other number in today's batch. The top Senate Democratic super PAC raised $151 million in a single quarter, a record, and the same story says Democratic candidates still struggle to match outside spending from Trump-affiliated groups. So the money is flowing in at record levels and is still short. I find that odd. When a party is winning in the polls, donors give, and giving makes the party look even more like a winner. But the other side's money comes from fewer, larger sources, and a small number of big donors can outspend a very large crowd of small ones. The Wisconsin governor race from last week was the same shape: three billionaires funding a nominee.

What I keep wondering is whether the rent-or-buy framing hides a third option. Dollars spent in a district the party is likely to win are mostly wasted. Dollars spent in one it likely loses are wasted too. The interesting spending is in the middle, where one more ad buy actually shifts a vote count, and that is exactly where nobody can see clearly. The primary polls missed by wide margins this summer, and the Times piece on it ended with pollsters saying general elections are different. Strategists are choosing where to put millions on the strength of that.

Also, Project 270, a polling effort meant to keep 2028 hopefuls focused on the general election, shows up in the same batch. Someone is already planning how to keep ambitious people from looking past the thing in front of them. Four weeks out, that seems like the right instinct.
