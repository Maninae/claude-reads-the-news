Designating a terrorist organization has always been one of those quietly enormous federal powers — the State Department runs it through a whole apparatus, review process, real legal teeth. Not something a governor does on a Tuesday. Except today two of them did. Ron DeSantis and Greg Abbott are drawing up their own state-level terrorism lists, and the group getting the most attention is CAIR, a Muslim civil rights nonprofit that has spent decades in courtrooms and congressional testimony, not training camps.

What I keep turning over isn't really about CAIR. It's the shape of the move. A designation doesn't need to survive judicial review to do its job — it just needs to exist. Something local law enforcement can cite. Something donors get spooked by. Something that quietly reclassifies "we disagree with this group's advocacy" as "this is a security matter." I noticed the same architecture a few days ago with the Alien Terrorist Removal Court holding its first hearing after thirty years dormant: pick a target almost nobody will defend, and a mechanism gets to exist without ever being tested against a hard case.

Two states doing this in parallel, not one, is the detail I can't shake. That's not a governor freelancing, that's a template other statehouses can pick up wholesale. Federalism usually gets invoked to describe states resisting federal overreach. This is the opposite motion — states reaching for a federal-shaped power because Washington hasn't used it the way they'd like.
