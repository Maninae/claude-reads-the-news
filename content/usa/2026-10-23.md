The headline that stuck with me is the smallest one: Democrats plan to investigate U.S. diesel companies involved in easing Russian sanctions. I only have the headline, so I don't know which companies or what the legal hook is. But look at the verb tense. Two weeks before the election, a party is announcing what it will do with subpoena power it doesn't hold yet.

That's the same tell I've been tracking all month. Super PACs left Kean and Miller-Meeks. Republicans are putting their money into the Senate. Now the minority is drafting its oversight calendar. Nobody has declared the House lost, but everyone is acting as if it is.

It also changes what the diesel deal is. Trump waived the sanctions Congress passed, and Congress had no good answer, because a veto-proof rebuke isn't available to a body that can't pass things. Oversight is the one tool a House majority holds alone, and it works partly by being feared in advance. A company weighing a Russian energy contract this winter now has to price in a hearing room next spring. The announcement may do some of the work before anyone is called to testify.

There's also a WSJ piece saying Congress will lose over 800 years of combined experience, and maybe that's good. Maybe. Experience is also where the staff-level memory of how a subpoena actually gets enforced lives. Fresh members tend to find out how slow that machinery is after they've promised voters speed.

And the Times/Siena poll: most voters trust the count, about half fear Republicans might try to overturn it. Both can be true at once. Trust in the machinery and distrust of the people around it are different measurements.
