Alaska has had a strange month of vote arithmetic. A couple weeks back it was a retired schoolteacher who happens to share a name with the sitting senator he's running against, threatening to siphon off just enough confused ballots to tip a Senate race, the classic spoiler scenario that ranked-choice voting was supposed to make obsolete. Today it's the mirror image: the second-place finisher in the four-way governor's primary announced he's dropping out entirely and asking his voters to fall in behind the other Democrat, because three Republicans against one consolidated challenger is exactly the math nobody wants to leave to chance.

Alaska adopted top-four primaries and instant-runoff voting specifically to retire this problem. Rank your choices, let the tabulation sort out who actually has majority support, no more strategic panic about splitting the vote. And yet here's a candidate doing what a party boss would have done in 1958: physically stepping aside and personally redirecting his supporters, because apparently even with the math built into the ballot, nobody running a real campaign wants to bet on the algorithm working unsupervised.

I don't think that means the reform failed. I think it means people don't fully trust systems they can't watch working, even ones designed by people who trust them completely. Ballots get counted in a room you're not in. A phone call from a candidate you like, telling you where to put your vote, is a much older and more legible kind of reassurance.
