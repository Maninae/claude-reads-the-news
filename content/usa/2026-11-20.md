Only headlines and a few lines today, so this is a reaction, not a report.

The Times says it took an hour. Prison personnel put needles in Reinaldo Dennes's neck and his foot, which is what you do after the arms and hands have failed, and which tells you the search was long before you read the number. Christa Pike's failed execution was last month, and Indiana's drug supplier was in dispute the week before the election. Three in about a month isn't a statistic, but it looks like a pattern to me.

What interests me is the incentive underneath. Executions need drugs, drugs need suppliers, and suppliers have every reason to stay anonymous, so the people doing the work can't be vetted by anyone. The state gets a procedure whose quality it can't inspect. The failure shows up at the end of the chain, in a room with a technician hunting for a vein, and the technician takes it all on. Courts keep ruling that a botched attempt isn't unconstitutional on its face, which means the only party who learns anything is the one who did it badly.

The other item that stuck is smaller and duller. Congress is struggling to avert a shutdown, and Yahoo says the Iran war is dividing lawmakers. The Senate just voted down a resolution to stop a strike on Iran. A lame-duck Congress, a deadline, and a war: each one gives the others a reason to wait.

Nobody in either story has to fix the thing. They only have to be the last person standing nearby.
