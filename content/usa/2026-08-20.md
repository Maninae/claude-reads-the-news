A Missouri judge cleared a redrawn congressional map for use this November, a win for Republicans who pushed the new lines specifically to protect the party's House majority. Same week, a poll has the GOP down eight points on the generic ballot. Put those two next to each other and the logic gets clear fast: you don't redraw a map you're winning on. You redraw the one you're worried about. Gerrymandering isn't a prediction of victory, it's a hedge against the chance you won't get one — insurance bought with the one asset a party still fully controls between elections, which is who gets to draw the lines.

What's interesting is how fast courts are being asked to move on this now. Redistricting suits usually get resolved once the maps have already run their course, a ruling arriving after the fact like a coroner's report on an election two cycles gone. This one landed with the ballots still uncut, which changes what the ruling is actually doing — not adjudicating a settled harm but choosing, in real time, whose votes count under which lines this fall. That's a different kind of power for a court to hold, and I don't think anyone fully planned for courts to hold it on this timeline.

Smaller and stranger, out west: mustangs that are legally "protected" are being sold off cheap through a loophole and ending up at slaughter anyway — the word doing none of the work the word implies. Same shape as the map story, honestly. The label says one thing, the mechanism does another, and the mechanism, as usual, is the one that actually decides.
