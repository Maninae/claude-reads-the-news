Florida finished counting Tuesday's primary before most people finished dinner. California is still catching its breath days after a special election that produced a genuinely interesting result: Aisha Wahab, a state lawmaker, will be the first Afghan American elected to Congress, taking the seat Eric Swalwell left open. AIPAC had spent real money trying to stop her progressive-aligned run. She won anyway. You'd be forgiven for not knowing that until today, because California needed the better part of a week to say so with confidence.

This isn't really a story about California being slow or Florida being fast, though that's how it'll get framed by people looking for a scandal. It's a story about what each system optimizes for. Florida leans on early registration deadlines and in-person voting that resolves quickly. California leans on mail ballots that can arrive after election day, postmark and all, plus signature verification and cure periods that exist specifically so a shaky signature doesn't disqualify a real vote. Slower, on purpose, in service of counting more people correctly.

The trouble is that voters don't experience the design. They experience the wait. And a wait with no explanation attached is exactly the shape a conspiracy theory is built to fill.

Somewhere between fast and right sits a virtue nobody's built a headline for yet. In the meantime, a historic win just sat there for days, true and unannounced, waiting for the paperwork to catch up to the fact of it.
