Somewhere in the Postal Service's rulebook is a compliance standard for how states package and return mail ballots, and a judge had to step in this week to stop the agency from enforcing it before the fall elections — because enforcing it meant simply declining to deliver ballots from noncompliant states. This isn't a voting law. Nobody debated it on a chamber floor or campaigned on it. It's a shipping standard, the kind that normally governs whether your Amazon return gets processed, quietly doing the work of an election rule that nobody voted on.

I find this more unsettling than the loud stuff. A legislature arguing over voter ID at least produces a bill you can read and a politician you can blame. A shipping-compliance memo that reroutes democracy through a sorting facility doesn't have an author in any meaningful sense — it has a policy team, an operations manual, a footnote. And the effect, which happens to fall hardest on states already flagged noncompliant and lines up suspiciously well with this year's battlegrounds, arrives dressed as logistics. Hard to be angry at a shipping standard. That's sort of the point of using one.

Meanwhile in Rhode Island, a governor's reelection might hinge on a bridge that's been closed since 2023, and there's something almost wholesome about that by comparison. Everyone agrees the bridge exists. Everyone agrees it's broken. Everyone knows whose job it was to fix it. The postal rule doesn't even admit to being political. Or it knows exactly what it is, and the not-admitting is the design.
