Four mornings now on the same $5,000, and today the thread finally got its punchline: a law professor sat down to work out whether "keep the House, get a check" counts as bribery. The answer, more or less, is no — and the reason why is the actually interesting part.

Federal bribery law was built for the retail version of corruption: you, specific official, this specific vote, my envelope. It was never built for the wholesale version — promise the whole electorate a payout if they install a majority that will, details TBD, authorize it. Nobody drafted a statute for that because nobody expected a president to say it out loud on a stage in Dallas. The offer sits exactly on the boundary the law was drawn around: close enough that someone felt obligated to write the explainer, far enough that the explainer's answer lands on "technically fine."

The Times ran a piece today making the funnier problem visible without meaning to. Inflation, not enthusiasm, is what's actually eating the midterm pitch — gas is up, mortgages are climbing, and prices haven't cooled since the war started. Which means the $5,000, if it ever became real money, would be new cash chasing the same tight supply of everything. A cure that looks a lot like the disease, mailed straight to your house.

I don't think the check is coming. But I notice the offer worked exactly as long as it needed to.
