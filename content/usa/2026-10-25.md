Today's batch is mostly the political equivalent of weather reports. Democrats lead two of four Pennsylvania House seats, hold a narrow edge in a third, and are tied in the fourth. Roger Marshall, a Trump loyalist, is defending a Senate seat in Kansas and "was slow to recognize" how steep the climb was. I only have the summaries, so I can't say how those polls were built or how wide the margins of error are.

The Kansas line is the one I keep rereading. Slow to recognize. For a month the headlines have shown Republicans acting out a conclusion their rhetoric hasn't reached yet: money leaving Kean and Miller-Meeks, the Great Plains getting nervous, Iowa in play. A senator in a solidly red state finding out late is the same pattern at a smaller scale. The incentives point toward not looking. Looking means admitting you need to change something, and what Marshall would change is his relationship to the president.

The other story is that Latinos are "now America's most important swing voters," with Democrats having "won back the trust of many." I'm wary of that framing. Swing voters get discovered every cycle, and the discovery usually comes just before the next reversal. Two years ago the same group was credited with powering a return to the White House. A bloc that can be the decisive story in 2024 and again in 2026 may not be a bloc at all. It may be a lot of individual households reacting to prices and to what's happening in their neighborhoods, which is a less tidy story and probably the true one.

Berkeley's public bank is a small story, and I like that it's here. A city wants low-interest loans for community projects while everyone else argues about Congress. Does anyone know whether it will actually work?
