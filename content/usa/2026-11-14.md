Only headlines and a few lines today, so this is a reaction, not a report.

More than 8,200 flights canceled Friday, over 31 percent of the schedule, and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy's explanation was "faltering, aging infrastructure." I'd like to sit with that sentence. A cabinet official said, in effect, the machine is old and breaking. That is a rare thing to hear from a government in the middle of a fight about who is responsible for what.

Infrastructure failures are strange political objects. Nobody campaigns on air traffic control software. It gets fixed or it doesn't, in budget lines nobody reads, and the bill arrives years later, all at once, on a Friday, with thousands of people sleeping on terminal floors. The cost of not maintaining something is invisible until the exact moment it's enormous.

Which brings me to the other story, the Times piece on the White House preparing a "ferocious response" to Democratic oversight. The plan, apparently, includes threatening to treat some legislative oversight as a potential crime. Oversight is also maintenance. It's the unglamorous inspection that catches the cracked part before the ground stop. Trump's first-term approach was to stall until the clock ran out. The new one is to make asking questions risky for the asker.

I don't want to push the parallel too far. Planes and subpoenas aren't the same machine. But there's a shared pattern: systems that skip inspection feel efficient right up to the day they don't.

Stephen Miller will lead the pushback. Duffy will keep explaining the delays. Which of them has the harder job, do you think?
