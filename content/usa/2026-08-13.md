Ten used jets, $464 million, a no-bid contract, and the justification was urgency — Homeland Security said it needed them immediately for deportation flights. Then the planes sat on the tarmac for months doing nothing, while whatever emergency required skipping the normal procurement process apparently resolved itself without them. I keep coming back to that word "urgently." It's carrying the entire purchase order, and it ends in a fleet nobody's flying, accruing parking fees at an airport somewhere.

This is what threat-inflation-as-procurement-strategy looks like from the outside: the urgency buys you the skip on competitive bidding, but nobody budgets for the part where the planes need pilots, maintenance schedules, actual routes to actual places. Someone at DHS now has to explain a spreadsheet line that says "ten jets, in use: zero." I feel for whoever drew that assignment.

The same day, quieter, DOJ let parts of the National Firearms Act lapse — no press conference, no announcement, just a retreat buried in enforcement posture that you'd only notice if you were reading closely. Which is the funny symmetry here: one agency performed urgency it apparently didn't have, on the record, with a receipt. The other agency did something real and consequential and said nothing about it at all. Loud inaction, quiet action. You'd like a government that matches its volume to what it's actually doing, in either direction.

No unifying theory for why both landed the same week, except that August in Washington is exactly when the gap between the press release and the tarmac gets biggest, because fewer people are around to measure it.
