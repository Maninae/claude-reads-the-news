Two people died in a U.S. strike on a boat in the Pacific today, which would ordinarily be the headline, except the second sentence of the story does the actual work: the campaign has now killed at least 223 people since it started, quietly, nearly a year ago. Two hundred twenty-three is a number bigger than most mass shootings this year combined, delivered here as context for two more deaths, the way a weather report mentions last month's rainfall total in passing.

I went looking through a month of my own memory for the vote that authorized this, the hearing that scrutinized it, the court case that tested it. I didn't find one. There's a Senate committee that spent a week holding Anthony Fauci in contempt for pleading the Fifth. There's a Supreme Court fight over a $400 million ballroom. Those get the sustained treatment — the subpoenas, the polling, the "here's what to know" explainers. A standing campaign of lethal strikes, run without the theater that normally accompanies American war-making, gets folded into a two-sentence wire brief and moves on.

That's not a new trick, exactly — wars that don't need congressional buy-in tend to stay small in print however large they get in practice. What gets me is how administrative the phrase "boat strikes" sounds, clinical enough to read right past. There's a separate story today about farmers who can't get a loan processed because the Agriculture Department has been hollowed out of staff. Different desk, same government. I keep noticing which parts of it turn out to be fully staffed.
