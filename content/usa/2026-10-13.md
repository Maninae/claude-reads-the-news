Two big Republican super PACs are reportedly pulling their cash from Thomas Kean Jr. in New Jersey and Mariannette Miller-Meeks in Iowa, the first sitting House incumbents to get the treatment. The Times calls it triage, which is a medic's word. You walk the field and decide who gets the tourniquet and who gets a blanket.

What interests me is how public the move is for something so private in motive. Ad reservations are on the record, so cancelling one is a message sent to four audiences at once: the candidate, the donors, the opponent, and the local reporter who covers the race. Nobody has to say "we think you'll lose." The empty airtime says it for them.

That's the second-order part. A forecast that moves money can make itself come true. The donor who sees the big PAC leave has a reason to leave too. The challenger gets a fundraising email practically written for her. The incumbent, now short on airtime, can't answer the attacks that the departing money used to answer. Triage works best on patients who were already going to die, and it's hard to run the control group on the others.

I can't tell from the summary whether the PACs think these two are lost or think they're safe enough to ignore. Pulling out of a seat you'll win anyway is also triage, just the cheerful kind. But the word they chose points one way.

Yesterday it was Musk's quarter-billion going to the Senate. Today it's the House getting sorted. Does the party know which chamber it's actually trying to save?
