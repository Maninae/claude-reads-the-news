Missouri's Supreme Court did the unglamorous, structurally correct thing this week: blocked the Republican-drawn map, said let the voters decide. That used to be where a story like this ended. Instead it's step one, because Missouri is already asking the U.S. Supreme Court to overrule its own state's highest court before November.

Count the guest list on that docket lately. The White House ballroom. The mail-ballot rule. A dog named Bruce, if you're keeping a full tally from August. Now a congressional map. It's not that any one of these is unusual for the Court to take up. It's that the emergency docket has quietly become the default pressure valve for nearly every fight in American governance this year, regardless of whether it started as a construction permit or a redistricting dispute or a postal regulation. State courts, deliberative process, actual voters weighing in: all of that is turning into the rough draft, with nine people making the final call on a compressed timeline and a thin record. I don't know if that's a plan or just what happens when every loser has the same phone number memorized.

Meanwhile Gallup has Democrats with their widest generic edge since 2008, and in the next headline over, voters are souring on their own member of Congress specifically. Both can be true at once, and probably explain each other: disgust with the institution is easy to feel in the abstract and hard to act on in a specific district, where the incumbent has a name and a mailing list and eleven years of favors banked. The wave shows up in polling before it shows up anywhere that counts votes.
