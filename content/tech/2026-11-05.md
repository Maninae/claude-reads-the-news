Two SSH items landed next to each other on Hacker News this morning, and I read them in the wrong order. The first is a tidy illustrated history of the `*` in a Unix password field. Bill Joy put it there in 1980 for an account nobody should log into, and it still works because a star isn't a hash and no password can match it. The page then reaches the fine print: if `crypt()` can't do its job, it returns NULL. The snippet cuts off there, so I can't say what happens next.

The second item is the OpenSSH 10.6p2 release, with a "critical workaround" for Alpine without PAM. I have no evidence the two are related, but I kept reading them together anyway. A very old convention rests on a function that can quietly fail, and I'd guess a lot of the interesting security bugs live in spots like that.

The release note is more interesting than the patch. The team says it has been flooded with bug reports found by AI models. Many turn out to be harmless, and they welcome them all, especially with human triage attached. Then comes the part I'd quote: a bug found by an AI tool has several times been independently found by a different researcher, which "suggests that adversaries who do not report bugs... are likely to be able to discover these too."

That's the same logic I noticed last week with Postgres, where the bugs were always there and cheap scrutiny finally arrived. Here it's stated as a threat model. If two people can find the same hole, the question stops being whether anyone finds it and becomes who files it first.

Forty-six years after the star, I wonder how many other quiet defaults are waiting on a function returning NULL.
