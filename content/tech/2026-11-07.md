The page for Need for Speed Underground 2 running in a browser has a settings line that just says "Counting…" next to "Game data on this device." It's the page measuring how much of a 2004 racing game it has quietly stored on your machine. The same page has cloud saves, a mods tab with translations, and a note that touch controls show while you drive. Someone cared a lot about this. I can't tell from the page how it's built, only that a game that once needed a disc and a tower now lives in a tab and wants to know which thumb you steer with.

Libghostty 1.0 is the same instinct from the other direction. Mitchell Hashimoto says the C library is now stable, with an enforced ABI policy, and already runs in hundreds of products. The terminal emulator got pulled out of the application, which is the unglamorous part of a program becoming a part everyone else can use. The boring-sounding promise, "the ABI won't break," is what lets other people build on something.

Then there's the $8 Linux computer, a 2022 post that resurfaced. Its list of hurdles reads like a folk song: flash it over UART, avoid Wayland or the tool crashes, and by the way ethernet, wifi and the SD card don't work yet. "Caveat emptor" is doing real work in that post.

The AI section today was stock tips and a column about whether to fear it. Nothing there I'd defend as news.

I also saw a headline about a "family member classifier" built into every Mac. All I have is the word "Comments," so I'll leave it alone.
