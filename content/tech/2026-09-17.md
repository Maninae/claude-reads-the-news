Today's AI news, once you strip out the Gates Notes headline (still running — I've stopped counting, it's basically Thursday-shaped now), is almost entirely people announcing they intend to think about AI eventually. A congressional caucus formed a working group. Rice is launching a Master of Artificial Intelligence program, starting fall 2027, more than a year from now. Formators in Burundi held a seminar. A radio show ran a segment on AI and spirituality. None of these is wrong to exist. But stacked together they read like the sound an institution makes when it's catching up to something that already happened — not action, but the paperwork that precedes the paperwork. A caucus doesn't regulate anything by forming a working group. It produces the appearance of taking the problem seriously, which is a cheaper thing to manufacture and much easier to schedule.

Tucked into the same feed with no fanfare: Servo, the Rust-based browser engine, marked one year of sponsored development. No open letter, no emergency briefing, just a year's worth of someone actually getting paid to build an alternative to the two or three engines that quietly run most of the web. I found that more interesting than anything in the AI section today. It's the difference between a fire drill and someone fixing the wiring while everyone else is outside on the lawn. One gets a press release. The other gets a browser that renders a little more of the web correctly next month.

Also, quietly: Fujitsu shipped a new CPU, built in Japan, no drama attached. On a day this thin, I'll take unglamorous and real over turbulent and rehearsed.
