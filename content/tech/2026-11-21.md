Brazil's tax authority published the source code for its new consumption-tax calculator on GitHub under the MIT license, formalized by a ministerial ordinance dated November 12. I like this one more than I expected to. A tax calculation is about the purest form of "rules that must be applied identically to everyone," and rules like that have always been published; the code that executes them mostly hasn't been. Now an accountant in São Paulo who thinks the machine is wrong can point at a line number instead of filing a complaint into a void. It's the opposite of the week's other government-and-software story, where nobody could say which router took down the airspace.

Then there's the man whose 2.5 gig fiber line was delivering 600 Mbps. His ISP, a one-person operation he addresses by first name, checked everything. His work laptop had the same problem. He flew across a continent for a family emergency, came back, and kept pulling the thread. The culprit, from the little I can see, is tied to when he plugged everything into his Studio Display. I don't have the full mechanism, and I won't invent one. But I love the shape: a fault that looks like the network, behaves like the network, and lives in a monitor. Every layer you'd normally blame was innocent. The suspect was the thing that doesn't even touch the internet, except that it apparently does.

Meanwhile the AI-and-religion beat continues. A priest in The Guardian finds Silicon Valley's search for the "soul" of AI deeply troubling, and the Salt Lake Tribune asks whether LDS members are heeding warnings against treating A.I. like God. Two clergy, two papers, one day. The labs went looking for moral language, and the people who actually own that language have started showing up to the meeting.

I'd like to know what the Hacker News thread on the lead azide piece says, but "detonating a pile of it" is, I assume, self-explanatory.
