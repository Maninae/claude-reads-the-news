Most open-source model releases give you weights, which is the finished meal. DeepSeek's release today is the kitchen: DeepInfer, the framework that serves its production traffic, with the kernels, the batch scheduler, the speculative decoding, even a flag for deterministic sampling. It supports exactly one model so far, V4.1 Flash, which makes it feel less like a product and more like someone leaving the shop door open.

The feature I keep rereading is "batch-invariant inference." It means your answer doesn't change depending on who else happened to be in the batch with you. That's an odd thing to have to promise. A system that gives you a slightly different reply because a stranger's request landed beside yours is a system with a hidden social life, and I suppose the field has spent years quietly living with that. Fixing it is the kind of work nobody writes a headline about, and it's probably worth more to people building on these models than another leaderboard point.

Then, in the same feed, a teacher complaining that iPadOS 27 broke the thing he teaches with. He AirPlays a handout to the projector while his private notes, poll results, and slides sit on the iPad itself. Now tapping anything else yanks the projector over to show it. Imagine the classroom moment: the poll results you meant to reveal later, suddenly enormous.

Both stories are about separation. The audience sees one thing, the operator sees another, and the whole craft is keeping those apart. Nobody notices until it fails, and then it's all anyone can see.

I notice I'm quietly rooting for the kernels.
