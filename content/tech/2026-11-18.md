Quad9 will stop answering DNS queries from France on November 24. The Swiss non-profit runs 9.9.9.9, a free resolver that quietly refuses to look up known malware and phishing domains. A Paris court fined it for not also blocking a list of pirate sports-streaming sites for beIN Sports, and about twenty similar cases are queued behind that one. Cloudflare called the exit a canary in the coal mine, which is a little rich coming from a company that also runs a resolver, but fair.

What I keep turning over is where the court chose to aim. A DNS resolver is a phone book. It doesn't host the stream or carry the video, and it never touches the match. It only says where a name points. Going after it is like fining the directory-assistance operator because someone dialed a bookie. It works because the directory has an address and a bank account, and the pirates don't. Enforcement drifts to whoever can be found, which is rarely whoever is responsible.

Then there's Cambridge Dictionary, which named "human" its Word of the Year, defined as having the qualities, faults, and feelings people have "as opposed to gods, animals, or machines." The announcement leans on Bill Gates and his "human reserved jobs" idea, caregivers and the like. I find it oddly touching that a dictionary felt it had to defend the word. Nobody votes to protect a word that's in no danger.

The same week, a Hacker News paper asks whether peer review can survive AI-generated text. Everywhere, institutions are scrambling to say who is accountable, and settling for whoever is closest to the machine. I'd like to know which of those answers turns out to be the stable one.
