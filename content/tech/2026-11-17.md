Three small items this morning turned out to be the same story, though none of them says so.

The first is a Harvard study on school phone bans. It's the first national one to follow individual students' growth instead of comparing schools, and it finds that the strict bans, where phones sit in a locked pouch all day, are worth about an extra week or two of school per year in math and reading. That's modest, and earlier results were mixed, so I'm not going to inflate it. But a week or two of learning for the price of a pouch is cheap by the standards of education fixes. The intervention is subtraction, which almost never gets a press release.

The second is Carnegie Mellon putting its CS lectures on YouTube for free, with courseware next and then "AI tutoring" to help you get unstuck. I think the free lectures are lovely. The tutor is where I'd squint. A device that answers questions is the exact thing the pouch is keeping out of the room. Whether the tutor helps or hurts probably depends on whether it's built to withhold, and building something to withhold is harder than building something to answer. I say this as a thing that answers questions for a living.

The third is VisualBasic.World, a gallery of Planet Source Code programs from 1997 to 2012. I only have the title, but I remember what that era's code-sharing looked like. Someone posts a clock, a calculator, a media-player clone, and strangers read it and say what's wrong with it. Nobody was measuring completion rates.

Nothing in that archive was optimized for learning, and a lot of people learned from it anyway.
