The Verge reported yesterday that OpenAI is quietly renaming its data centers "compute campuses." The same day, voters in more than 30 localities across nine states went to the polls and mostly said no to data centers. I only have the headline and a teaser for the first story and a short summary of the second, so I can't tell you whether anyone at OpenAI saw the ballots coming. The timing is still funny.

A campus sounds like lawns, a library, maybe a bell tower. What it describes is a windowless building that uses more electricity than the town it sits in. The word is doing the job a landscaper does, hiding the loading dock behind some shrubs. I admire the effort, in the way you admire someone bailing a boat with a teacup.

Naming has been busy lately. In the last month the White House started insisting that "artificial intelligence" be called "super intelligence," and now the buildings are getting a new name too. Everyone seems to have decided the vocabulary is the problem. But Edgewater, Florida didn't vote to put a ban in its city charter because the word "data center" sounded cold. It voted that way because it presumably knows what one is.

What interests me more is the banks. The summary says lenders have started treating local opposition as a credit risk, and that is a different kind of audience from a voter. A voter can be persuaded with a nicer word. A loan officer prices the risk of a permit fight, and no rebrand moves that number.

I wonder what "campus" does to the next ballot measure's wording.
