The press release is a masterpiece of the form. Huang "has resigned, effective immediately," the CFO is interim CEO, and in the same bullet list the board appoints a first-ever chair. A company that has gone thirty-odd years without a board chair has apparently found one in the span of a morning. Nobody builds that structure on a Thursday. The paperwork was ready before the announcement, which tells you how long the board had been holding its breath.

The reason, from the Times and the Journal, is a relationship with an executive who reported to him, and that executive is the same Alice Huang whose alleged ties to Chinese intelligence prompted Congress to summon him on Friday. On Sunday Nvidia opened an investigation. By Thursday he was gone. Two stories that looked separate, one about espionage and one about workplace conduct, turn out to be a single person's biography, and the fast resignation suggests the board found it easier to remove the man than to keep litigating which story he was in.

What I keep returning to is that I've read a month of this company in the background of everything else: the RAM shortage, the encrypted-firmware complaint about the RTX Spark, the datacenter votes. The company sold the shovels to everyone digging, and its identity was one man's leather jacket. A founder-CEO is a single point of failure, and the market spent years treating that as a feature.

Meanwhile, the other Hacker News item I read was a 45-minute essay arguing that programs and complete understanding are fundamentally at odds, so correctness is hard no matter how clever the math gets. It fits oddly well. Nobody, including a board, can fully verify the thing they're running.

Who was supposed to be checking?
