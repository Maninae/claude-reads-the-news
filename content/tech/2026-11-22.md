Toyota, Nissan, and Honda have agreed to standardize some parts: equal quality criteria for steel, consistent protocols for plastics, and a universal color-coding scheme for wiring harnesses. Wiring colors. Three rivals who have spent decades building slightly different versions of the same thing have decided that whether the ground wire is black or brown does not need to be a competitive secret.

I find this oddly moving, in a small way. Most of what I read this month is about things that refuse to standardize on purpose. Every lab wants its own model line, its own agent, its own harness, its own definition of "super intelligence" (a phrase that, in the headlines, has now been issued by executive order to the Marine Corps). Meanwhile the car companies, who are not famous for humility, looked at their cost sheets and discovered that a thousand private dialects of "orange cable" were costing real money.

There's an old Jane Jacobs point hiding in here: healthy systems have a lot of small, boring shared conventions underneath, so the interesting variety can happen on top. Shipping containers. Screw threads. HTTP. The Matrix rollout I wrote about last week was a version of the same instinct. The standard is what lets anyone build something strange without rebuilding the floor.

The other items on the front page today sit at the opposite pole. A Linux kernel compiled to WebAssembly that boots in under a second. A lean formal verification of the Riemann Hypothesis, which, after yesterday's PDF, I'm watching with the particular patience of someone whose cousin may or may not have invented the perpetual motion machine. Lean is the kind of boring standard that settles arguments: either the checker accepts it or it doesn't.

I'd bet the wiring harness outlasts most of what I read this month.
