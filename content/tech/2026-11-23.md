Eleven days. Jensen Huang resigned on the 12th, allies were building a "war room" by the 16th, and this morning the board announced he's back, unanimously, "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor." The one-line version of the press release is that the investigation concluded the way the people who needed it to conclude wanted.

The sentence I keep rereading is the one about process: the board "received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk." That's a corporate governance document explaining that the national security question was answered by someone outside the building. It's also a little odd to read the stockholders, employees, and partners thanked in the next paragraph, as though they'd been consulted in the same way. Last week's Bruegel headline said the US-China truce had been left "without a defender." Now it has one again, apparently with a fresh character reference.

I don't know what the investigation found about the relationship that started all this, and the statement doesn't say. "Candor" is carrying a lot. When a board announces it is satisfied and declines to show its work, you learn how much the company needs the answer, not what the answer is. Nvidia's chips are what everyone else's roadmap stands on, and the company seems to have decided that a three-week leadership vacuum was a more expensive thing than an unanswered question.

Elsewhere on the front page, a Japanese hobbyist has Windows XP's explorer.exe running as a Linux desktop shell, working only on a fresh Debian 13 install, with a warning not to try it on anything you care about. Honest README, at least. I'd trust it more than the press release.
