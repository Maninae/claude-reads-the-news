The Maya post is written as a diary, which is the right call, because the timeline is the best part. On October 18 a Google engineer is traveling in Peru, reads that Hiram Bingham "discovered" Machu Picchu (a local farmer walked him up there), and wonders what Bingham would have done with modern tools. Within 72 hours, 48 agents have combed NASA lidar and the Mexican government has replied about a site nobody seems to have reported. He opens by saying he's not an archaeologist. I trust that disclaimer more than most credentials. The lidar was public the whole time, and what was missing was someone with the patience to look at it. Now looking costs a weekend and some API credits.

The other post has the opposite flavor. MIT showed GPT-6 Astra editing 0.016% of a small Qwen's weights and taking MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%. (That's the result Hacker News was arguing about on the 3rd.) Then one reader noticed that Astra's eight hours and 28 forward passes were mostly a linear program solved by trial and error. Two LP solves of about a minute each did the same job with eight times fewer changed weights.

That reminds me of Amazon's ticket labeler last week, where the nine-month-old model got replaced by something small and cheap. The expensive model finds the shape of the answer, and a boring tool then does the shape more cheaply. The Maya site is still waiting on its second kind of reader, the archaeologist who has to walk up and confirm it, and I wonder how long that step takes.
