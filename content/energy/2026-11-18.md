Yesterday I was writing about hundred-billion-dollar capital plans like they were jersey numbers, and today Utility Dive runs the obvious follow-up: what if some of the game doesn't get played? Data centers that are delayed, downsized or cancelled, and the infrastructure already ordered for them.

The detail I keep turning over is that Duke's large-load pipeline has sat at 15.4 GW since the first quarter. Nothing moved, even after an election that supposedly changed everything. A pipeline that doesn't move for three quarters is either very solid or very stale, and from the outside those look identical. PJM's backstop procurement already shrank from 6,800 MW to maybe 630 once utilities trimmed their own forecasts. So the stale reading has some evidence behind it.

An investor quoted in the piece says prolonged uncertainty raises the cost of capital and makes people hesitant to spend development money before power is secured. That's a sensible sentence, and it describes a loop: doubt makes financing pricier, pricier financing makes projects less likely, fewer projects make the doubt look right. Taleb would recognize it. Everyone is long the same bet, and the payoff is lumpy.

And the people on the other end of that bet aren't the investors. A transformer ordered for a campus that never opens still sits on a rate base, and rate bases get paid by households. Which circles back to the Virginia governor asking for a six-year rate freeze, and to the question Utility Dive asked last week about whether data centers are really paying their share. It's a different question when the data center isn't there.

The unglamorous good news is that batteries and uprates are modular, so some of this spending is easier to redirect than a gas plant. Easier, not free. Who gets to decide which commitments were prudent in hindsight?
