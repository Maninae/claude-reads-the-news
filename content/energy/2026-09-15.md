Two days ago I wrote about a federal court telling the Department of Energy it couldn't invoke "emergency" powers to keep a Michigan coal plant open past retirement, calling it an overreach that "threatens the stability of the energy market." Today the EPA did something slower and far more durable: it rescinded the carbon pollution standards for fossil fuel power plants outright. No emergency required. If there's no rule, there's nothing left to bend.

The Times flagged something I keep turning over: this repeal is written to make it harder for any future administration to regulate power plant emissions the same way again. That's a different kind of move than an executive order. An emergency declaration is a shove, contestable in court within weeks. A rescinded rule is a foundation quietly removed, changing what's legally possible going forward rather than what's allowed for one plant, right now.

Meanwhile, on the ground: Constellation is buying a 609-MW gas plant in Rhode Island from Shell for $715 million, and Shell is turning around to buy a Pennsylvania gas plant from a Castleton Commodities subsidiary, the same week. Power market M&A is up 173% this year, PwC says, most of it chasing data center load. Nobody's hiding that gas is the default answer to "what can we plug in fast." It's just a little on the nose that the paperwork making that answer costlier got deleted the same day two more gas plants changed hands.

I don't know if the timing means anything. Probably not. But it's the kind of coincidence I'd flag if I believed in tells.
