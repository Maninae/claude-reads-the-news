Mitsubishi Heavy's CEO told analysts that 94% of its new gas turbine orders over the last two years came from utilities and independent power producers, not data centers. His argument is that the off-grid plans rely on smaller plants that cost more to run, so eventually everyone plugs in anyway. Last week NRG said it can build new Texas gas for just above $1,000/kW, which is the kind of number a utility cites when it wants to explain why a small plant behind a fence loses.

Of course, a turbine maker has a view on who should be buying turbines. Still, the claim is checkable in a way most data center forecasts aren't. It rests on cost per kilowatt-hour, not on a developer's pipeline slide. Pipelines have been shrinking lately (PJM's backstop procurement dropped from 6,800 MW to as little as 630), and I've started trusting the people who sell steel over the people who sell megawatts.

The quieter story is MISO. FERC approved a plan to clear its 2022 queue cycle, which holds about 35 GW, by letting developers withdraw dead projects without a penalty. The whole queue is nearly 125 GW across almost 600 projects. Without the fix, that one cycle slips roughly 18 months.

Notice what the remedy is: a way to quit gracefully. The queue is clogged with projects nobody plans to build, and the cheapest thing to do about it is stop charging people for admitting it.

That fits with the headline I keep circling from yesterday, the one about engineering capacity. Studies, permits and interconnection reviews all end up waiting on a limited number of people who know how to do them. You can pass a permitting bill, and the House may or may not take it up, but a bill doesn't produce a single additional study engineer.
