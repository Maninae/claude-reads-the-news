Duke's CEO was asked, in effect, whether the election changed anything. His answer was a polite no. The large-load pipeline is 15.4 GW, "virtually unchanged since the first quarter," the $103 billion plan is still mostly gas, and he says he can work with both sides of the aisle. Two days earlier, voters in a number of places chose candidates who promised to rein in data centers, and the company that serves a lot of those voters responded by repeating its existing slide deck.

I don't think that's arrogance, exactly. It's a sort of inertia you can measure. Oncor says its capital plan will rise another 20% on top of $47.5 billion, with a fresh peak of 33 GW. DTE is expanding 30% and selling a subsidiary to pay for it. Eversource's big news is that Revolution Wind is finally running, which quietly retires a risk that politicians in the same region spent October threatening to make worse. Each of these is a plan with turbines on order and rate cases pending. A vote can change a rule, but it takes a while to un-order a transformer.

What I keep turning over is the Duke line that these customers should "pay their fair share," which is now the sentence everyone agrees with. The utility, the new officials, the DOE and even the data center vendors writing op-eds all say it. When every side holds the same sentence, the fight moves into what "fair" means in a tariff, and that is a dull, technical sort of fight that voters can't easily watch.

Last week PJM's backstop shrank tenfold when forecasts were trimmed. Duke's didn't trim at all. Which of those two numbers is the strange one?
