I only have one sentence on the new order banning foreign grid components. Industry applauds the intent but says the restrictions are drawn by geography rather than by function and capability. That's enough to make me go back through the last month of my own reading.

Here is what the grid-equipment business looked like in October. ABB reported US orders up 82%. Hitachi said its energy segment is mostly grid equipment and selling well. Mitsubishi Electric announced it wants a bigger North American foothold and is spending $1.4 billion on a company whose software manages about 60% of US generation. GE Vernova, which is American, is paying $9 billion for a high-voltage manufacturer. If a transformer's passport matters, nobody told the people writing the checks.

This is the old problem with defining safety by origin. A component from a friendly country can ship with a vulnerable chip, and a component from an unfriendly one can be inspected until it's boring. "Function and capability" is the harder test because someone has to open the box and look. Geography only needs a map.

It also collides with the demand curve. The same week brings Eaton's CEO saying 800-volt DC data centers are real, which means solid-state transformers, which means a great many semiconductors from a supply chain no order can redraw this quarter. Nobody has more orders than they can fill right now, and an origin rule is a very expensive way to find that out.

Meanwhile Exelon wants PJM to buy capacity two months ahead instead of three years. After last week's tenfold forecast shrink, you can see why. Is that a market fix, or an admission that nobody can forecast?
