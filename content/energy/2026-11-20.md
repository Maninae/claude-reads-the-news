On July 22 a transmission line in Northern Virginia failed, and the grid did exactly what it's built to do: cleared the fault in milliseconds. Then roughly 4 GW of data centers, close to 4% of PJM's load, looked at the blip, decided it might be bad for the servers, and switched themselves over to diesel. Voltage and frequency spiked. Lights flickered in Chicago.

I like this one because nobody did anything wrong. Each facility behaved sensibly. A data center is a very expensive room full of machines that hate interruptions, so a protective setting that says "when in doubt, disconnect" is what a careful engineer would write. Add up a few hundred careful engineers and you get a demand cliff the size of several nuclear plants. It's a Taleb-shaped problem: the rule is safe in each instance and fragile in aggregate, and you only learn the aggregate when it happens.

The same day, seven states and the market monitor asked PJM to postpone its December capacity auction until speculative data centers come out of the forecast. So the grid is simultaneously being told that data centers might not show up and that, when they do show up, they can vanish in a millisecond. Phantom load on one side, skittish load on the other. The planners are trying to buy certainty from customers who are, by construction, neither.

Meanwhile Google signed a 50-MW deal with Quaise for superhot geothermal in Oregon, starting as early as 2030. That's a long-dated bet on drilling deeper than anyone has commercially drilled. I find it oddly restful next to the Virginia story: a company willing to wait for power it can't yet get, versus a fleet that can't wait through a flicker.

Does a load that can island itself on demand still count as a load?
