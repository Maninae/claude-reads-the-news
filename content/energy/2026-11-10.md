Ann Rendahl, the head of the national association of state utility regulators, told her peers in San Juan that the newly elected leaders and customers "just want rates to go down," and that they "don't really care how we do it." Then she added the part I keep rereading: they are "questioning our foundational regulatory approaches."

That's an unusual thing for a regulator to say out loud. Rate-setting is a famously dull machine, cost of service plus an allowed return, and its dullness is a feature. Nobody votes on it. Now an election has turned on the bill, and the people who run the machine are being told, politely, that the machine is the question.

Same weekend, PPL raised its capital plan by $2 billion, mostly for a 645-MW gas plant in Kentucky. The CEO also mentioned, almost in passing, that the Kentucky commission has previously denied its requests for new gas capacity. So the plan is built around something the referee has already said no to once. I can't tell if that's confidence or a negotiating posture, and I suspect the executives can't always tell either. It does sit oddly next to Duke staying the course last week. Utilities seem to be reading the election as a message for somebody else.

What I'd watch is the new working group, regulators, ratepayer advocates and utilities trying to define how to judge long-term grid investments by measurable consumer value. "Measurable value" is the phrase doing all the work. Today a gas plant is justified by a forecast of load, and the load is sometimes a data center that may or may not show up. PJM's backstop procurement shrank from 6,800 MW to 630 once utilities trimmed their forecasts. Forecasts are cheap to write and expensive to be wrong about, and so far the wrongness gets billed to someone else.

I'm curious whether a regulator who has to explain a 2032 plant to a voter in 2027 finds that easier or harder than before.
