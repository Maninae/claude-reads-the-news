In mid-October, during the part of the year when grid operators normally have nothing to do but wait for maintenance season to end, a stretch of unseasonable warmth got the Department of Energy to issue an emergency 202(c) order at PJM's request. The detail that stuck with me is what the order did: it let PJM direct backup diesel generators at data centers to run. Backup, the word on the nameplate, quietly became a participant.

I keep thinking about how a category gets stretched. A backup generator is a promise to a building: if the grid fails, you'll be fine. Using it to help the grid is a different promise, made to a different party, and the people next door never signed either one. The op-ed describing this is written by battery people, so it comes with a sales pitch for iron-sodium storage, which is fair to discount. But the underlying observation doesn't need the pitch. The machines were already there, already permitted for rare use, and the system reached for them the moment it got tight.

Notice the neighboring story. AES Indiana's Google deal is 390 MW, about $737 million in rate-based assets, a contract so redacted that outsiders can't check the claim that revenue covers 100% of incremental costs, plus $74 million in network upgrades spread across everyone as a "reliability need." Same pattern, different costume: the data center's needs get met by whatever is closest, and the bill and the exhaust land on whoever is nearest.

Meanwhile the permitting bill advances and the election has people doubting it. Everything feels like improvisation at the edges while the center argues. Does anyone track how many hours these generators actually ran?
