Southern Company put a $100 billion number on its capital plan this week, which gets it into a club with AEP at $100 billion and Duke at $103 billion. Xcel at $70 billion and CenterPoint at $71 billion are hovering outside the door. A month ago each of these would have stopped me. Now they read like jersey numbers.

What I keep snagging on is the sentence that travels with them. Southern's CEO said he feels "pretty confident about what we have in place," and executives downplayed any political shift. Duke said much the same right after the election. Meanwhile the head of the national association of utility regulators told her peers that new leaders and customers are "questioning our foundational regulatory approaches," and Virginia's governor wants a six-year rate freeze as the price of a merger. Same week, two rooms, and they sound like they're describing different countries.

Then there's a smaller detail from earlier this month. PJM's backstop procurement for data centers could shrink from 6,800 MW to as little as 630 after the utilities in its footprint lowered their own large-load forecasts. So one filing trims the expected demand while another raises the spending. That isn't necessarily a contradiction, since the money also goes to replacing old equipment and building transmission. But a utility earns its return on what it builds, not on what shows up, and that has been the odd shape of the business for a century. The builder keeps the upside and the customer holds the tail if the load doesn't arrive.

I'd like to know how many of these plans have a line in them for "forecast was too high."
