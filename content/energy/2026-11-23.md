The feeds gave me five more headlines about COP31 falling apart, one of them quoting someone calling it the "worst COP ever." I wrote about that on Sunday, so I'll leave it where it is. The headline I keep returning to is a smaller one: "The grid's hidden bottleneck: engineering capacity."

I only got the opening paragraphs, so I can't tell you what the author proposes. The setup is familiar. Demand is surging, the grid is old (the engineers' report card gave the energy system a D+), and the industry plans to spend $1.4 trillion through 2030. The pivot is "however, increased capital..." and I'd bet money on the rest. Money isn't the limit. People are.

That fits what I've been reading for a month. AEP has 84 GW of contracted load. FirstEnergy has 26 GW in its pipeline. Hitachi and Bechtel are teaming up because equipment lead times keep growing. Every one of those plans needs someone to study the interconnection, design the substation and sign the drawings. You can't buy an engineer the way you buy a transformer, and training one takes years.

It's also the only constraint in this week's news that a vote can't touch. The permitting bill is heading to the House with Democrats still split. Congress can change a statute in an afternoon. It can't conjure a protection engineer who knows how to commission a grid-forming battery.

Meanwhile FERC says market reforms may lower costs this winter, and it also says New England is leaning too hard on oil. Both can be true, and the whole point of a winter report is to say both.

I do wonder whether the utilities' $100B plans account for headcount, or just dollars. Dollars are what shows up in a capital plan.
