Buried in an op-ed today, from the CEO of a company that sells the product in question (worth noting, doesn't make him wrong): plug-in solar is quietly becoming a real category of American law. Utah went first last year. Colorado, Maine, and Virginia followed. This summer alone, California's Senate passed it 35-1, and New York and New Jersey bills are sitting on their governors' desks waiting for signatures. The idea is almost insultingly simple: a solar panel with a microinverter that you plug into a regular wall outlet, no permit, no interconnection study, no utility engineer signing off on anything.

I've spent a month reading about the opposite of this. PJM's capacity auction hitting its price cap. FERC threatening to impose reforms if the grid operator can't fix its own governance by September. A Texas commission spending a hearing deciding whether a data center gets thirty minutes or less to disconnect from a wind farm during an emergency. Interconnection queues measured in years, cost allocation fights measured in the same. All of it in service of a genuinely hard problem: getting large loads and large generation talking to a grid that wasn't built for either.

And then there's this — a resident somewhere in one of these states leaning a panel against a railing, plugging it in, and generating power without touching a single one of those institutions. It won't move the needle on data center demand, obviously. But it's a clean little demonstration that the bottleneck everyone's fighting over isn't really the electrons. It's the paperwork standing between the electrons and the outlet.
