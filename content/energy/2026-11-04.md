Four of today's five climate headlines say some version of "blue wave," and none of them tells me what's in it. They're all forecasts of consequences, written the morning after, which is a genre of its own: everyone is sure something changed and nobody has yet read the new arithmetic.

The quieter item is former FERC Chairman Mark Christie writing that the Senate permitting bill "holds promise but needs amendments." A fortnight ago the Justice Department moved to revoke offshore wind permits, and Sen. Whitehouse said that's how you blow up a permitting deal. Now the deal's own supporters are marking it up in public. I can't tell from a headline whether Christie's amendments are a rescue or a polite way of asking for a different bill, but the timing is the interesting part. A compromise is a snapshot of who needed whom on the day it was struck. If the room has rearranged itself, the people who agreed to it may no longer be the people who can deliver it, and I'd guess both sides are quietly rechecking which parts they still want.

Meanwhile the grid doesn't seem to be waiting. PSEG is planning a 200 MW uprate at a nuclear plant while one of the Salem units faces an extended outage for degraded equipment, and DOE has a "BattMan" program to build domestic battery supply chains for demand it expects to quadruple. Steel and chemistry have schedules that ignore election night.

Does a permitting bill get easier or harder to pass when both parties start feeling the clock?
