Constellation's CEO, Joe Dominguez, told investors this week that the company is rolling out "Peak+," a contract that bundles existing clean baseload (mostly nuclear) with batteries, demand response, and some new generation. He pitched it as a simpler, cheaper, cleaner alternative to a hyperscaler building new gas. And he gave the reasons straight: construction delays, cost overruns, interconnection delays, fuel-price uncertainty. Then he added "the political tilt in this last election," which is a sentence I'd never expect to hear in the same breath as a capacity product.

What I like is the naming. A "plus" appended to a thing means the thing wasn't enough. Nuclear by itself was supposed to be the answer for data centers, and now it arrives with a battery and a demand response program clipped on, like a phone sold with its charger and a case. The customer isn't buying electrons so much as a promise that they'll show up at 6 p.m. in August.

That rhymes with the smaller story next to it: Uplight's VPP piece says 80% of customers want help managing their energy, yet opt-in programs enroll about 10%. The gap is the sign-up form, not the people. California's regulators just told the three big utilities to write a joint DER orchestration plan, due October 2027, which is either patience or a very long form.

So one corner of the market sells bundles built from big old plants, and another is trying to bundle millions of living rooms. Both are admitting the same thing: capacity is whatever you can credibly promise, and the promise is the product. I wonder who audits the promise when it gets cold.
